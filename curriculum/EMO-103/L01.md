# EMO-103 / L01 — Triggers and appraisals

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Explain triggers and appraisals in the context of emotional regulation.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall EMO-101's distinction between an emotion, bodily experience and action. A fictional learner receives a short message saying 'Can we talk tomorrow?' What information is known, and what meaning might the learner add?

## Core teaching

A trigger is a circumstance or experience that precedes or is associated with an emotional response. An appraisal concerns how a situation is evaluated in relation to goals, needs, expectations or perceived coping resources. Appraisals may be rapid and not fully conscious. Emotional responses can involve experience, bodily changes, expressive behaviour and action tendencies; these components need not change together. A feeling can be real even when an initial interpretation is incomplete or inaccurate. Distinguish the fact of experiencing an emotion from evidence about another person's intent.

Gross's process model conceptualises emotion generation through interaction among situations, attention, appraisal and responses. It is an analytical model, not a claim that emotions always occur in a clean linear order. Feedback matters: an action can change the situation, and bodily experiences can influence subsequent attention or interpretation. Context and past learning also matter. Avoid interpreting every response as a deliberate choice or suggesting someone causes their own distress by thinking incorrectly. Material threats, injustice and unsafe conditions require appropriate action, not merely an altered appraisal.

The goal of regulation literacy is to understand opportunities for constructive action without judging emotions as failures. Emotion and action are distinct: anger may signal a perceived violation, but it does not dictate aggression; fear may accompany uncertainty but does not establish whether a situation is dangerous. A learner can identify a tentative appraisal and examine alternatives while preserving safety. Serious symptoms or persistent impairment warrant qualified care. The fictional examples in this course are not diagnostic exercises or instructions to revisit traumatic experiences.

## Framework

**Event–attention–appraisal–response map**

Separate what happened from what was noticed, what meaning was inferred, and the reported response or action tendency. Mark unknowns and distinguish observed behaviour from inferred intent. Identify one point at which clarification, a safe change in circumstances or a different response could help. Include a safety check before proposing reinterpretation.

## Worked example

A fictional volunteer receives 'We need to discuss the schedule' from a coordinator. They notice the lack of a greeting, interpret it as dissatisfaction and report feeling anxious. The event is the message; the interpretation is an inference; the reported emotion is real but does not confirm dissatisfaction. Alternative explanations include routine scheduling, a brief writing style or a time constraint. A constructive option is to ask whether preparation is needed and continue ordinary responsibilities. If previous messages contain credible threats, the context changes: safety and support take priority, rather than assuming a harmless meaning.

### Contrasting case

If an official notice explicitly states that a venue is closed for safety reasons, treating concern as merely a mistaken appraisal could be dangerous. The event supplies concrete evidence of a constraint; an appropriate response respects it.

## Guided practice

**Annotate a fictional sequence.** A teammate sends a revised task list with one assignment removed. The learner thinks 'They no longer trust me', feels upset and wants to withdraw from the discussion. Identify event, attention, appraisal, response and action tendency; write two unknowns.

**Worked solution.** Event: revised list; attention: removed assignment; appraisal: inferred distrust; response: reported upset; action tendency: withdrawal. Unknowns: why the task was removed and whether the change is permanent. A proportionate clarification asks about the change without asserting motive. An alternative explanation is redistribution of workload. This does not require the learner to deny the emotion or claim the alternative is true.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify one specific correction and apply it to an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning, evidence, safe boundaries and task quality rather than personal characteristics or emotional states.

## Independent transfer

Write a novel fictional event–appraisal–response map with two plausible interpretations and a safety exception. Describe one possible feedback loop, such as how a response might change subsequent information.

# EMO-103 / L02 — Alternative interpretations

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Compare and distinguish alternative interpretations in the context of emotional regulation.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the event–appraisal distinction. A fictional colleague has not replied to a routine request. How could several interpretations fit the same fact, and what would justify choosing among them?

## Core teaching

Cognitive reappraisal involves changing how a situation is interpreted in order to influence its emotional meaning. It differs from pretending an event did not happen, suppressing outward expression or inventing a comforting story. A useful interpretation remains compatible with available evidence and can change when new evidence appears. The goal is not automatically to replace negative interpretations with positive ones. Sometimes the most accurate interpretation is unpleasant and calls for practical action. The learning skill is generating and testing alternatives without dismissing a person's experience.

Research distinguishes reappraisal from expressive suppression and reports differing associations and consequences across studied settings. These findings do not imply that every reinterpretation is beneficial or that restraint of expression is always harmful. Regulatory flexibility emphasises fit between strategy and context. An interpretation that helps tolerate an unavoidable wait may be useful; reinterpreting repeated breaches of an agreement as harmless can prevent a necessary conversation. Assess whether a claim is known, plausible, unlikely or unresolved, using available facts rather than an arbitrary optimistic probability.

Apply intellectual skills from INT-105: seek disconfirming evidence, identify assumptions and consider alternative explanations. Also distinguish a possible explanation from an excuse. A missed commitment might result from an emergency, unclear expectations or disregard; discovering the cause can guide the response but does not erase the impact. Respect other people's privacy when seeking clarification. In situations involving coercion or abuse, do not prescribe empathy for the person causing harm or reinterpret threats as misunderstandings. Prioritise safety and specialist advice.

## Framework

**Fact–hypotheses–evidence–response table**

Write the observed fact and initial interpretation. Generate two genuinely different plausible hypotheses, including one that preserves the possibility of a real problem. List evidence for and against each, identify missing information and choose a proportionate response that works under uncertainty. Review after obtaining clarification. Do not assign certainty unsupported by the facts.

## Worked example

A fictional project partner misses an agreed deadline without explanation. The first interpretation is 'They don't care about my time.' Alternatives are an unexpected obstacle, misunderstanding of the deadline, or a pattern of disregarding commitments. The deadline and missed output are facts; the motivation is unknown. A proportionate response is: 'The draft was due yesterday and I need it to finish the comparison. Can you confirm when it will be available?' If the response reveals a genuine obstacle, renegotiate. If repeated delays persist, raise the impact and establish a workable agreement. The reinterpretation does not require accepting unreliable behaviour.

### Contrasting case

A fictional manager repeatedly issues documented threats. Suggesting that the learner must find a positive explanation could obscure credible harm. The appropriate response is to seek safe advice, follow relevant procedures and protect safety. A balanced interpretation is not an obligation to assume good intent.

## Guided practice

**Compare alternative interpretations.** A fictional friend cancels a planned meeting twice. Information: one cancellation included a reason; the second did not. Write three interpretations, evidence that would distinguish them, and a communication response that does not require certainty about intent.

**Worked solution.** Possibilities: temporary conflicting responsibilities, uncertainty about the arrangement, or a lower priority for meeting. One reason is known but the broader pattern is unresolved. Ask whether a different arrangement is realistic and state the impact of repeated cancellation. If the person cannot commit, adjust future plans without diagnosing their character. The response protects the learner's time and respects the friend's choice.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify one specific correction and apply it to an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning, evidence, safe boundaries and task quality rather than personal characteristics or emotional states.

## Independent transfer

Develop a fact–hypotheses–evidence table for a novel fictional conflict. Identify a plausible uncomfortable interpretation and a plausible benign one. Draft a response that is appropriate under both, plus the information that would change it.

2. Why generate more than one interpretation?

4. When should reappraisal not replace practical action?

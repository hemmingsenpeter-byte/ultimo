## Core teaching

Emotion regulation concerns processes that influence which emotions occur, when they occur, or how they are experienced or expressed. It is broader than calming down. Gross's process model distinguishes strategy families often described as situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change and response modulation. These are categories for analysing actions, not a prescribed sequence. Choosing a quieter meeting place is situation modification; attending to the specific issue rather than an unrelated concern changes attention; reconsidering a plausible interpretation is cognitive change; choosing how to express a feeling concerns response modulation. Avoiding a dangerous situation may be appropriate, while avoiding every ordinary difficult conversation can have costs.

Regulation involves goals and context. A short pause before answering may be useful if a later discussion is feasible; postponement may be unsuitable when immediate safety action is needed. Expressive restraint can protect privacy or allow someone to respond thoughtfully, but it should not be confused with eliminating an emotion or being obligated to hide distress. Research on regulatory flexibility considers noticing contextual demands, having multiple strategies available and adjusting after feedback. No one strategy guarantees a good outcome in every case.

Safe everyday options include seeking clarification, changing a feasible aspect of a situation, taking an ordinary comfortable break, or asking for support. They are not treatments for mental health conditions. If difficulties are persistent or impairing, professional assessment may be appropriate. Do not use deliberate distress induction, breath restriction, punitive exercises or forced disclosure. If a situation involves immediate danger, the priority is safety and emergency support. The objective is to select proportionate responses that respect the person's goals and agency.

## Framework

**Goal–context–options–feedback matrix**

Define a meaningful immediate goal: safety, accurate understanding, respectful communication or manageable functioning. Identify controllable aspects of the situation, legitimate duties and available support. Generate at least three options across strategy families. Compare likely benefits, costs, timing and safety; select one or two compatible actions. Set a review condition. The matrix is an original educational planning tool, not a validated treatment.

## Worked example

A fictional learner receives unexpected criticism while presenting a proposal. They want to understand whether the concern is about evidence or format. Options include requesting the specific example (situation modification), directing attention to the relevant question (attentional deployment), recognising that a critique of the document may not be a judgement of their worth (cognitive change), and choosing a respectful reply while still feeling disappointed (response modulation). They ask for clarification and note a follow-up question. If the criticism becomes threatening or discriminatory, different support or formal procedures may be needed. A successful response is accurate and safe, not proof the learner stopped feeling upset.

### Contrasting case

A fictional worker hears an emergency warning during a difficult discussion. Continuing the conversation in order to practise regulation would be inappropriate. Respond to the safety requirement first. A strategy suitable for an ordinary disagreement does not override emergency procedures.

## Guided practice

**Select a strategy mix.** A fictional student receives ambiguous feedback and has a deadline tomorrow. Compare (1) immediately sending an angry reply, (2) briefly postponing a non-urgent reply and requesting clarification, (3) ignoring the feedback until the deadline passes. Describe benefits, costs and conditions for each.

**Worked solution.** Immediate reply risks escalation if intent is uncertain, though urgent factual clarification may be necessary. A short pause plus specific question can preserve time and accuracy if the deadline allows. Ignoring all feedback risks missing required work. A suitable choice might ask what needs revision and agree a realistic response window; use an accessible format if needed. Do not claim that any option eliminates emotion.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify one specific correction and apply it to an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning, evidence, safe boundaries and task quality rather than personal characteristics or emotional states.

## Independent transfer

Use the matrix for two fictional cases: an ordinary disagreement with a flexible deadline and an urgent legitimate responsibility. Select different strategy mixes, state why and identify feedback that would prompt a revision.

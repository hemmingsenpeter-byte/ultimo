# EMO-103 / L04 — Constructive responses

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Evaluate and justify decisions about constructive responses in the context of emotional regulation.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the goal–context–options matrix. A fictional team member has a legitimate grievance about an agreed task. How can they express it constructively without having to feel calm or obtain agreement?

## Core teaching

A constructive response is an action or communication that addresses a legitimate goal while respecting safety, evidence, autonomy and other people's rights. It is not defined by whether everyone agrees or whether an unpleasant emotion disappears. Distinguish emotional experience from expression and chosen behaviour. A person may feel angry and communicate a clear concern without insults, or feel anxious and ask a necessary question. Regulation is not a requirement to hide feelings or accept unfair treatment.

Constructive communication can separate an observable event, its concrete impact, a need or goal, a specific request, and the next appropriate action if the issue remains unresolved. These components are practical teaching choices, not a universal script that works in every culture or relationship. Use proportionate language and verify assumptions. A request permits a response; a demand backed by coercion may undermine autonomy. In organisational situations, formal obligations or authority can legitimately constrain choices, but they should be communicated transparently and through appropriate channels.

Consider timing and power. A direct conversation may be feasible between peers in a safe context, while a formal reporting route or specialist support may be safer when there is harassment, coercion or a serious power imbalance. Do not require confrontation. Acknowledging another person's perspective does not mean accepting harmful behaviour. If a response causes unintended harm, review the facts, take appropriate responsibility and revise. Avoid grading people by how quickly they forgive, how much emotion they express or whether they successfully persuade others.

## Framework

**Observe–impact–request–review**

Check safety and authority. Describe the observable issue without guessing motive. State its practical impact and the legitimate need. Make a specific feasible request, allowing questions and alternatives where appropriate. Agree a review point or identify an authorised next route. Evaluate accuracy, respect, safety and follow-through rather than the other person's compliance.

Text model: observed issue → shared understanding → request/decision → review; disagreement may remain.

## Worked example

A fictional project partner repeatedly submits figures after the agreed deadline, delaying a combined report. A constructive response might be: 'The figures arrived after our agreed Tuesday deadline in the last two cycles. I could not complete the combined report until Thursday. Can we agree an earlier submission time or flag delays by Monday?' The partner explains that a required input is often unavailable. Both may agree to change the schedule or raise the dependency with the responsible coordinator. If no agreement is possible, the learner can document the dependency and use an appropriate escalation route. The communication does not assert that the partner is careless or require the learner to suppress frustration.

### Contrasting case

If a person faces credible threats from someone they depend on, a polite direct script is not necessarily safe. The goal becomes accessing appropriate safety support and considering options with qualified assistance. The course does not prescribe confrontation or an individual exit plan.

## Guided practice

**Simulate two responses.** Fictional issue: a team member publicly dismisses an accurate contribution. Draft a response for a safe peer context and an alternative for a context with a significant power imbalance. Identify an observable fact and a desired outcome.

**Worked solution.** In the safe peer case, describe the specific statement, explain that it prevented review of the evidence, and request discussion of the figures. In the power-imbalance case, consider a trusted support person or appropriate formal channel rather than direct confrontation. Preserve relevant evidence lawfully and without sharing private data in Ultimo. A strong answer avoids motive claims, identifies safety conditions and permits disagreement.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify one specific correction and apply it to an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning, evidence, safe boundaries and task quality rather than personal characteristics or emotional states.

## Independent transfer

Complete a fictional conflict simulation using the event map, alternative hypotheses, strategy comparison and constructive response. Include a contrasting case where direct communication is inappropriate, then revise after feedback on accuracy, autonomy and safety.

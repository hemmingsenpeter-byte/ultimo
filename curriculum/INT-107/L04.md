# INT-107 / L04 — Quantitative claims

**Draft — independent review pending.** Outcome: Evaluate and justify decisions about quantitative claims in the context of mathematics and science literacy.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve percentage points and graph scale. A headline says an intervention cut mistakes by 50%. What must you know before interpreting it?

## Core teaching

A quantitative claim needs a defined numerator, denominator, population, time frame, baseline and comparison. Absolute and relative differences answer different questions. A risk declining from 2 in 1,000 to 1 in 1,000 halves the relative rate but reduces the absolute rate by one per thousand. Sample size and uncertainty matter: small counts are often volatile. Average values can conceal differences in distribution; weighting matters when combining groups with different sizes. Distinguish descriptive claims from causal conclusions. A numerical result can be arithmetically correct yet irrelevant to a decision if it measures a proxy or excludes important costs. Recalculate from the underlying figures where available and mark assumptions rather than silently filling gaps.

## Framework

Use a quantitative claim audit: reconstruct the calculation; check comparable definitions and denominators; express absolute and relative effects; examine uncertainty and missing groups; distinguish association from causation; state the decision relevance. Do not interpret precision as reliability.

## Worked example

A fictional team reports 90% success in a 10-job pilot and 80% in a 100-job comparison group. That means 9/10 and 80/100. Combining them yields 89/110 ≈ 80.9%, not the unweighted mean of 85%. The pilot's higher observed rate is not conclusive evidence of superiority because assignment, job difficulty and sampling uncertainty are unknown.

### Contrasting case

A statement that 90 of 100 users completed a specified task is a valid descriptive claim if the count and population are accurate. It need not demonstrate a treatment effect.

## Guided practice

Compare 10 failures per 1,000 jobs with 6 per 1,000. Model: 1% versus 0.6%, a 0.4 percentage-point absolute drop or 40% relative reduction; four fewer failures per thousand. Identify one omitted metric such as cost and one rival explanation such as easier jobs.

Attempt before reading the model; compare your reasoning and correct a specific gap. Record assumptions and ask what would change your conclusion.

## Independent transfer

## Knowledge check

1. What is the absolute reduction from 2% to 1%?
2. How should group averages be combined?
3. Does a statistically described difference prove causality?

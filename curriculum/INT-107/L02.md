# INT-107 / L02 — Linear and exponential models

**Draft — independent review pending.** Outcome: Compare and distinguish linear and exponential models in the context of mathematics and science literacy.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve dimensional analysis. Compare adding 20 customers each month with increasing a customer count by 20% each month.

## Core teaching

A linear model y = a + bt adds the same absolute amount b per unit of time. An exponential model y = a × r^t multiplies by the same positive factor r at each time step. For growth, r exceeds 1; for decay, r lies between 0 and 1. A fixed percentage increase acts on the current value, not the original base. This creates compounding. A short segment of exponential growth can look nearly linear, so examine changes and ratios over several periods. Model fitting is not explanation: a convenient curve does not establish the causal mechanism or guarantee future growth. Real systems may saturate, encounter constraints or change regimes. Compare plausible assumptions before extrapolating.

## Framework

Make a table at t = 0, 1, 2, 3. Compute consecutive differences and consecutive ratios where defined. If differences are constant, test a linear model; if positive ratios are constant, test an exponential model. Label initial value, growth parameter, unit of time and validity range.

## Worked example

Two fictional reading groups start with 100 participants. A adds 20 each month: 100, 120, 140, 160. B increases by 20% per month: 100, 120, 144, 172.8 modelled participants after three months. Counts must eventually be interpreted as estimates rather than literal fractional people. At first the paths are similar, but compounding widens the gap.

### Contrasting case

A community group with only 250 eligible members cannot indefinitely increase its membership by 20% monthly. A constant exponential model eventually violates the population boundary.

## Guided practice

Compare a stock starting at 200 that loses 20 units monthly with one declining 10% monthly. After two months the linear stock is 160; the multiplicative stock is 162. After three months they are 140 and 145.8. Explain why the second model loses a smaller absolute amount each period.

Attempt before reading the model; compare your reasoning and correct a specific gap. Record assumptions and ask what would change your conclusion.

# SOC-104 / L01 — Confidence versus skill

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Explain confidence versus skill in the context of social confidence.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall MEN-107's distinction between process and outcomes. A fictional learner gives an accurate introduction while feeling nervous. Another feels confident but interrupts repeatedly. What can confidence and skill tell us separately?

## Core teaching

Social confidence is commonly used to describe a person's perceived ability or ease in social situations. Self-efficacy is a more specific theoretical concept concerning beliefs about capability to perform actions in particular contexts. Bandura's work distinguishes efficacy expectations from outcomes; feeling capable is not the same as demonstrating the skill or obtaining a desired response. A person can communicate effectively while uncertain, and someone can feel confident while making avoidable errors.

Skill concerns observable performance against relevant criteria. In conversation, these may include a clear introduction, appropriate turn-taking, checking meaning, respecting refusal and communicating the intended information. Criteria should fit the context, accessibility needs and role rather than reward volume, eye contact, spontaneity or a particular personality. Introversion, quietness and a preference for written interaction are not defects.

Confidence and skill may interact, but their relationship is not deterministic. A learner with a skill gap may benefit from instruction and feedback; a capable learner who feels uncertain may prefer a low-stakes opportunity or support. Structural barriers, language and unclear expectations can affect both. Do not diagnose social anxiety from nervousness or assume everyone needs to become more outgoing. Persistent distress or functional impairment may warrant professional support. The course evaluates task-specific communication skills in fictional cases, not the learner's self-esteem.

## Framework

**Perceived capability–observed skill–context matrix**

Record a fictional task and the learner's reported expectation, keeping it separate from observable performance. Identify the relevant criterion, resources and constraints. Compare evidence of skill with perceived capability; select instruction, an accessible practice opportunity, structural support or no change as appropriate. Review using a novel task. Do not score confidence as competence.

Text matrix: high/low perceived capability × demonstrated/not yet demonstrated skill; each cell suggests questions, not a diagnosis.

## Worked example

A fictional learner introduces a community activity accurately, asks whether the recipient wants more information and respects their refusal. The learner reports feeling uncertain. The performance meets the stated task criteria despite low perceived confidence. Another fictional learner reports high confidence but interrupts the recipient and provides the wrong time. The second needs feedback on accuracy and turn-taking, not praise for confidence alone. In the first case, optional practice may be useful if wanted, but nervousness does not invalidate the demonstrated skill.

### Contrasting case

A person prefers communicating through a written message and provides all relevant information clearly. A rubric requiring spontaneous speech and sustained eye contact would measure an unrelated style unless those behaviours are genuinely necessary and justified for the task.

## Guided practice

**Analyse four fictional cases.** A: capable and confident; B: capable and uncertain; C: confident but inaccurate; D: uncertain and missing instructions. Identify the appropriate learning response.

**Worked solution.** A may proceed to a new equivalent task. B demonstrates the current skill; optional supported practice can be offered without requiring more confidence. C needs specific correction of inaccurate or disrespectful conduct. D needs access to instructions and skill teaching before judging performance. None establishes a mental health condition.

Now select two observable criteria for an introduction: state the activity accurately and ask whether further information is wanted. A criterion such as 'seems charismatic' is too vague and may reward irrelevant style. Explain why an equivalent written response could demonstrate the same objective.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Identify a specific reasoning or communication correction and repeat using an equivalent fictional case. Assess observable task quality, not personality, popularity or another person's compliance.

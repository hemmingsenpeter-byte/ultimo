# EMO-107 / L04 — Support options

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Evaluate and justify decisions about support options in the context of grief and vulnerability.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the loss–restoration timeline and permission framework. A fictional bereaved friend has difficulty arranging everyday tasks. Which kinds of support address practical needs, and when might qualified care be more appropriate?

## Core teaching

Bereavement support may include practical help, ordinary companionship, community or cultural resources, peer groups and professional care. Each addresses different needs. Assistance with transport, meals or administrative tasks may be welcome, but should be offered rather than imposed. Someone may want company today and privacy tomorrow. A support offer should be accessible, feasible and appropriate to the person's circumstances, rather than judged by how extensive it is.

Health New Zealand provides public information about grief and loss and ways to access further help. A primary-care provider or qualified mental health practitioner can assess persistent distress, functional difficulty or other health concerns; specialist bereavement services may provide additional support. Someone can seek qualified support when wanted, without meeting a classroom duration threshold. Do not diagnose prolonged grief, depression or another condition using emotional expression, time since death or a worksheet. Serious physical symptoms should not be attributed to grief without appropriate medical assessment.

A peer or tutor can listen, offer practical assistance and help identify available services. They cannot provide clinical treatment, guarantee eligibility or availability, or assume sole responsibility for someone's recovery. Respect refusal of ordinary help unless an immediate safety or actual safeguarding obligation requires action. In New Zealand, 111 is the emergency number for immediate danger; other locations require their own emergency route. Confirm current contact and eligibility details before sharing a specific non-emergency service. This exercise assesses decision-making and referral literacy rather than grief outcomes.

## Framework

**Need–choice–route–review**

1. Identify the communicated need and urgency.
2. Offer appropriate practical, social, cultural or professional choices.
3. Check consent, access, availability and the helper's actual role.
4. Agree follow-up only when wanted and feasible.
5. Escalate clinical concerns to qualified support and immediate danger to emergency services.

Text model: ordinary need → feasible voluntary support; clinical question → qualified care; immediate danger → emergency assistance. More than one route may be appropriate.

## Worked example

A fictional neighbour is managing most tasks after a bereavement but has difficulty getting to an appointment. They do not want to discuss the loss. A neighbour might offer a ride if feasible or help identify accessible transport. The person accepts transport information and declines conversation. Later they report persistent difficulty functioning and ask about additional support. The neighbour can offer help finding a primary-care or bereavement service and let the person choose whether to make contact. The first offer does not require disclosure, and the second does not imply a diagnosis or that grief should have ended.

### Contrasting case

A helper insists that a bereaved person attend a weekly group to prove they are recovering. A group may be useful when wanted, but mandatory participation and fixed progress milestones are not justified by this course.

## Guided practice

**Match four fictional cases to routes.** A: transport to an appointment. B: information about a community gathering. C: persistent difficulty with ordinary functioning and a request for help. D: immediate danger. Identify an option, access requirement and boundary for each.

**Worked solution.** A: voluntary practical help or accessible transport; do not assume acceptance. B: invited community contact; respect privacy and verify local details. C: qualified health or specialist bereavement support; offer access assistance without diagnosing. D: emergency services promptly, not a scheduled conversation. Multiple routes can coexist, and qualified care need not replace ordinary companionship.

Consider a variation: the person in A declines the ride because another arrangement is preferred. Accept that choice rather than interpreting it as rejection of support. In C, if no appointment is available, identify an alternative qualified route; do not substitute the helper's own treatment advice.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning or communication gap and repeat with a novel fictional case. Assess task quality, not personal emotional experiences.

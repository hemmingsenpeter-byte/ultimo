# EMO-107 / L03 — Voluntary disclosure

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Apply voluntary disclosure in the context of grief and vulnerability.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall EMO-106 on consent and boundaries. A fictional friend says they do not wish to discuss a recent loss. What distinguishes a supportive invitation from pressure to disclose?

## Core teaching

Vulnerability here refers to allowing some aspect of one's uncertainty, feelings or needs to be known. Disclosure is a decision about what information to share, with whom, when and for what purpose. It is not a compulsory demonstration of authenticity, trust or learning. Someone may explain that they need a schedule adjustment without describing the circumstances of a loss. The appropriateness of disclosure depends on trust, role, safety, culture, privacy and likely consequences.

WHO psychological first aid guidance emphasises humane, practical support that respects dignity. The crisis-support principle of listening without pressure can inform ordinary peer communication without making the peer a clinician or certified responder. A useful invitation offers options: conversation, practical assistance, another contact or no immediate engagement. Respect a refusal. Do not demand a narrative as proof of need or assume everyone must talk about loss to recover. A listener's curiosity is not itself a legitimate reason to collect information.

Disclosure may not be reversible, especially in digital systems or public organisations. Consider the minimum information necessary and who can appropriately receive it. A peer must not promise absolute confidentiality when real safety or safeguarding obligations may apply. Explain relevant limits honestly. Do not place identifiable bereavement stories, health records or private messages in Ultimo or its tutor. If someone reports immediate danger, emergency procedures take priority. For other concerns, a qualified professional or trusted community support may be preferable to ordinary peer conversation. The objective is appropriate choice and consent, not the amount of emotion expressed.

## Framework

**Permission–purpose–scope–choice**

Ask whether help or conversation is wanted. Clarify the legitimate purpose and minimise information requested. Offer choices of format, timing and whether to continue. Explain applicable confidentiality limits. Respect refusal and offer an alternate route if wanted. For immediate danger, use emergency assistance.

Text model: voluntary invitation → consent or refusal → appropriate response → review only if agreed. No detailed narrative is required for ordinary practical help.

## Worked example

A fictional student informs an educator that a family loss has affected their deadline and asks about options. The educator needs to understand the requested change, not the details of the death. An appropriate response is: 'We can discuss the deadline and available options. Would you prefer written information about the process?' If the provider requires particular documentation, the educator explains the actual requirement and who receives it rather than inviting an unnecessary personal account. The student can choose privacy and still receive practical guidance. If they want support beyond the educator's role, the educator can help identify an appropriate service.

### Contrasting case

A fictional group requires every participant to recount a painful experience before receiving ordinary practical information. This creates unnecessary disclosure pressure. An accessible alternative would teach with fictional examples and allow participants to opt out of discussion.

## Guided practice

**Rewrite three fictional invitations.** A: 'Tell me everything or I can't help.' B: 'You have to talk about it to recover.' C: 'If you would like company or help with a task, I'm available this afternoon.' Identify each consent or privacy issue and rewrite where necessary.

**Worked solution.** A makes help contingent on unnecessary disclosure; ask what kind of practical help would be useful. B makes an unsupported universal claim and pressures expression; offer a choice rather than a recovery rule. C is appropriately bounded if the offer is genuine and refusal respected. For any case in which a role limits confidentiality, explain this before inviting sensitive information where possible.

Now consider a fictional written message containing private details the recipient did not need. The appropriate response avoids forwarding or copying it, follows applicable procedures and clarifies the minimum information required for the task. Do not store or reproduce actual messages in this exercise.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning or communication gap and repeat with a novel fictional case. Assess task quality, not personal emotional experiences.

## Independent transfer

Write a fictional exchange in which someone declines to discuss a loss. Include a practical offer, an alternative contact if wanted and a privacy boundary. Explain how the response changes if immediate danger is reported.

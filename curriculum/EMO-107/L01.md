# EMO-107 / L01 — Grief variability

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Explain grief variability in the context of grief and vulnerability.

## Orientation and retrieval

Imagine a fictional bereaved adult who enjoys an ordinary activity in the afternoon and feels sad that evening. Recall MEN-105's account of recovery as variable adaptation. Why is it misleading to interpret this change as failure to progress through a universal sequence?

## Core teaching

Grief refers to responses to significant loss. Bereavement generally refers to circumstances following a death; mourning describes personal and socially situated ways of expressing or observing loss. These terms overlap in ordinary language. Emotional, cognitive, bodily and behavioural changes can occur in different combinations. Sadness, longing, numbness, anger, relief and enjoyment of ordinary activities may coexist or vary over time. A person's visible reaction does not establish the importance of a loss or how much support they need.

A common error is to treat stage models as a mandatory ordered sequence. Bereaved people need not pass through a fixed series of emotions, and a stage label cannot establish whether someone is adapting appropriately. Stroebe and Schut's dual-process account distinguishes demands associated with attending to the loss from demands associated with reorganising everyday life. It proposes movement between loss-oriented and restoration-oriented processes. This is a model for understanding variation, not a schedule allocating particular activities or emotions to specific days.

A useful support plan respects the person's choices, changing circumstances and access to resources. Practical responsibilities can continue alongside grief, and ordinary enjoyment need not mean the loss is forgotten. Persistent distress or functional difficulty can warrant qualified assessment, but someone can seek help whenever they want. Duration alone, visible emotion and a classroom worksheet cannot diagnose a grief-related condition. Immediate danger or medical emergencies require appropriate emergency help. No learner should revisit a personal loss to demonstrate this competency.

## Framework

**Loss–restoration–support timeline**

1. Describe the fictional loss and the limited information provided.
2. Identify possible loss-related and changed-life demands.
3. Record variation without assigning mandatory stages.
4. Identify optional practical, social, cultural and professional support.
5. Review what the person wants and what has changed; retain uncertainty.

Text model: changing demands and resources across time ↔ changing support needs. The timeline is descriptive, not a test of recovery or a prescribed schedule.

## Worked example

A fictional adult loses a close relative. During one week they arrange a family gathering, deal with changed household responsibilities, and attend a familiar recreational activity. A reminder later brings sadness. The gathering might involve attention to loss; the household arrangements concern changed practical responsibilities. The recreational activity might provide ordinary enjoyment or respite. None of these actions establishes a stage or clinical status. A friend can offer help with transport and ask whether company is wanted. The person may accept one offer and decline another. If responsibilities become persistently difficult and the person wants additional support, help them locate a suitable qualified provider rather than assigning a diagnosis.

### Contrasting case

A fictional person declines to discuss a death at work. Silence does not establish denial, a lack of affection or a particular stage. Respect the choice and offer practical accommodations if wanted. Another person who expresses sadness openly is not automatically coping worse.

## Guided practice

**Analyse a fictional seven-day record.** Monday: memorial planning. Tuesday: arranging household administration. Wednesday: spending time with a friend. Friday: sadness following a reminder. Sunday: requesting help with a practical task. Identify which activities might relate to loss or changed responsibilities, what remains unknown, and two possible offers of assistance.

**Worked solution.** Memorial planning may involve both mourning and practical arrangements. Administration concerns changed responsibilities; time with a friend may be meaningful or provide ordinary company. Friday's sadness does not indicate a reversal of progress. Sunday suggests a practical need, not a clinical conclusion. A feasible offer could be transport or help identifying an appropriate service, if wanted. Unknowns include the person's preferences, other demands and how manageable daily life is.

Now compare two fictional helpers: one insists the person attend a group before receiving practical assistance; the other asks what help is wanted. The second respects agency. A support group can be a valid choice without becoming compulsory.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning or communication gap and repeat with a novel fictional case. Assess task quality, not personal emotional experiences.

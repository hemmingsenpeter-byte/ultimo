# MEN-104 / L01 — Compulsion and impairment

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Explain compulsion and impairment in the context of compulsive behaviour.

## Orientation and retrieval

A fictional person checks a locked door repeatedly even after confirming it is locked. Another checks a door twice because a work procedure requires two independent checks. Before reading, explain why counting repetitions alone cannot distinguish these situations. The goal is recognition of possible impairment and appropriate support, not diagnosis.

## Core teaching

A habit is a learned tendency to perform an action in a familiar context. An enjoyable repeated activity, a routine and a compulsion are not interchangeable concepts. In clinical descriptions of obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), a compulsion can be a repeated action or mental ritual performed in response to distressing thoughts or felt rules. Obsessions are recurring, unwanted thoughts or experiences; a person may experience obsessions, compulsions or both. The meaning, function and clinical context matter. A person with a meticulous routine does not necessarily have OCD, and a person need not have visible rituals to need help.

Impaired control describes difficulty governing a behaviour in accordance with one's intentions or circumstances. Functional impairment means interference with important activities or responsibilities, such as learning, relationships, work, finances or self-care. WHO descriptions of gambling and gaming disorders include impaired control and persistence despite adverse consequences; these are specific clinical categories, not a licence to label every frequent digital activity an addiction. Similar outward actions may have very different explanations. Distress, impairment and persistence are reasons to consider professional assessment rather than proof of a particular condition.

Distinguish physical dependence, compulsive symptoms and addiction. Physical dependence is a bodily adaptation that may produce withdrawal when a substance or medicine is changed; it can occur during appropriately prescribed treatment and does not by itself establish addiction. MEN-104.L04 addresses withdrawal boundaries. Avoid treating any of these phenomena as lack of character. This course is designed to build literacy, identify the limits of a peer's role and support access to qualified help. No learner should be asked to disclose private symptoms or conduct a diagnostic screening.

## Framework

**Function–control–impact–context map**

1. Describe the behaviour neutrally: what is observed, not a label.
2. Identify the stated purpose or experience, if voluntarily provided; mark unknowns.
3. Distinguish reported difficulty controlling the behaviour from frequency alone.
4. Note reported effects on ordinary functioning and relevant context, without assigning severity scores.
5. Select a proportionate response: ordinary conversation, professional assessment or urgent help if immediate danger is present.

Text model: behaviour + context + reported impact → support question, not diagnosis. This original worksheet is not a screening tool.

## Worked example

A fictional apprentice says they spend a substantial part of each evening repeating a checking ritual, feel distressed if interrupted and increasingly miss ordinary commitments. Their colleague can notice the interference and offer to help locate a qualified health professional. They cannot conclude whether the apprentice has OCD, another condition or a different explanation. A second fictional apprentice plays a game regularly, enjoys it, maintains responsibilities and chooses when to stop. Frequency alone provides no sufficient basis for a disorder label. The support decision in each case follows the reported impact and needs rather than an arbitrary time threshold.

### Contrasting case

A laboratory worker repeats an equipment check because an authorised safety protocol requires it. Repetition is appropriate in that context. Conversely, someone may experience distressing mental rituals without any visible repetition. Neither observation is sufficient to diagnose a condition.

## Guided practice

**Case comparison.** Classify the information in three fictional records: A, an employee follows a required two-person verification process; B, someone reports being unable to stop a repeated activity despite missed commitments; C, someone takes a prescribed medicine and asks whether withdrawal is possible. For each, separate observation, unknowns and support action.

**Worked solution.** A: routine has a stated safety function; no impairment is reported. B: reported control and impact justify a supportive offer of qualified assessment; do not assign a diagnosis. C: medication questions belong with the prescriber or pharmacist; dependence and addiction are not equivalent. The exercise does not require the learner to ask about actual substance use or symptoms. A strong answer describes what would warrant urgent escalation without inventing an individual clinical history.

Attempt before consulting the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning gap and retry with an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning and safe boundaries, not the learner's own health or circumstances.

## Independent transfer

Construct two fictional descriptions with similar repetition but different functions and impacts. Produce a neutral comparison and one appropriate referral option. Identify three pieces of information that are unknown rather than speculating.

## Safety and accessibility

Draft education only, pending independent source verification and qualified mental-health, safety and accessibility review. Work with fictional or synthetic cases only. Do not diagnose, provide treatment, change substances or medicines, deliberately provoke distress, or assess fitness for safety-sensitive duties. No personal disclosure is required. Written, oral, tabular or diagrammatic responses are equally acceptable; any rehearsal is voluntary and may be stopped. Seek qualified professional assistance for persistent impairment; use local emergency services for immediate danger. Keep identifiable evidence out of Ultimo.

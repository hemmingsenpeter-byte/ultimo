# MEN-104 / L03 — Support pathways

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Apply support pathways in the context of compulsive behaviour.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall why reported impairment can justify support without establishing a diagnosis. A fictional friend says they are worried about their repeated behaviour but do not know whom to contact. What useful assistance can a peer provide without taking over?

## Core teaching

Support pathways are routes to suitable assistance. Informal support can provide listening, companionship or practical help; organisational support can address access or workload barriers; primary care can assess health concerns and coordinate referrals; specialist services may provide evidence-based treatment for particular conditions. These routes are complementary. A peer is not expected to identify the diagnosis or choose a clinical treatment. The individual's preferences, cultural context, accessibility, safety and service availability matter.

Different problems can require different expertise. A person reporting distressing rituals may benefit from an appropriately qualified mental health professional familiar with OCD; a person concerned about gambling harm may prefer a specialist gambling service; someone worried about substance use may need addiction and medical services. Treatments and suitability are assessed by clinicians. Public sources describe therapies for OCD, but reading a treatment description does not qualify a learner or AI tutor to administer it. Avoid a one-size-fits-all recovery philosophy or promising that a particular programme will work.

Supportive communication begins with permission, observable concerns and a practical offer. Avoid shaming, interrogating, making threats or promising to keep dangerous situations secret. In Aotearoa New Zealand, Healthify's directory provides links to addiction services, including gambling support. Verify the current local details before sharing them; emergency situations need emergency services rather than a routine helpline. Respect an adult's autonomy except when immediate safety or applicable safeguarding duties require escalation.

## Framework

**Ask–identify–offer–handover**

Ask whether support is wanted. Identify the type of need without assigning a diagnosis. Offer a small practical step: find an official directory, arrange an accessible appointment if requested, or identify a trusted contact. Handover clinical questions to the appropriate professional. Clarify what to do if danger becomes immediate. Follow up only as agreed; do not become the person's sole support.

A fictional support map should include preferred route, alternative route, access barrier, consent and urgent exception.

## Worked example

A fictional friend says their repeated gambling is affecting household bills. A supportive reply might be: 'You mentioned this is affecting your bills. Would you like help finding a service that works with gambling concerns?' If they agree, the peer helps identify a specialist service and asks whether phone, text or in-person contact is preferable. The peer does not demand access to banking, diagnose a disorder, prescribe abstinence or take financial control. If the friend raises immediate danger, the peer follows local emergency procedures. Financial or practical assistance can be considered through appropriate services without replacing clinical support.

### Contrasting case

A friend who offers unsolicited treatment instructions and demands daily proof of compliance may undermine trust and autonomy. Conversely, listening alone may be insufficient when serious harm or immediate danger is disclosed. The appropriate action is neither coercion nor passive dismissal.

## Guided practice

**Build a support pathway.** Consider three fictional people: A reports intrusive thoughts and repeated rituals; B is worried about gambling harm; C is concerned about changing alcohol use and reports possible dependence. For each, identify a professional route, a practical accessibility option, a peer boundary and urgent exception.

**Worked solution.** A: qualified mental health assessment; offer help finding an accessible appointment, no exposure exercise. B: gambling support service and, if relevant, qualified financial guidance; no access to personal accounts demanded. C: medical or addiction assessment before changing use; no withdrawal plan. In all three cases immediate danger requires emergency assistance. Success depends on matching needs to expertise while respecting the person's choices.

Attempt before consulting the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning gap and retry with an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning and safe boundaries, not the learner's own health or circumstances.

## Independent transfer

Create a fictional support map with at least two suitable routes and one accessibility barrier, such as language, transport or appointment availability. Draft a brief supportive response and a plan for checking current local service information. Include a role boundary.

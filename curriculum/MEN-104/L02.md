# MEN-104 / L02 — Contextual triggers

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Compare and distinguish contextual triggers in the context of compulsive behaviour.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the function–control–impact distinction. Suppose a fictional person spends more than intended on an activity after encountering promotional messages, but behaves differently when with friends. What can context explain, and what remains unknown?

## Core teaching

A contextual trigger is a circumstance that precedes or is associated with an urge or behaviour. Context can include environmental access, cues, routines, social situations, emotional states, advertising or practical constraints. Antecedent means something occurring before an event, not necessarily its cause. Consequence means what follows. Repeated associations can help someone or a professional investigate patterns; they do not by themselves establish a mechanism or diagnosis. A trigger is not a moral defect or a reason to blame a person for experiencing difficulty.

Separate an external cue from an internal experience and from the broader environment that makes a behaviour easy or difficult. For gambling, WHO's public-health account includes commercial availability, promotion and wider social and economic circumstances. A response that focuses only on individual self-control misses those structural influences. An ordinary consent-based environmental adjustment may be suitable, such as opting out of promotional notifications, but a clinical symptom pattern may need specialist guidance. Do not deliberately expose someone to feared cues, induce urges or instruct someone to resist a ritual as an educational experiment.

An observation map is useful for organising fictional facts and considering proportionate support, not for proving what a person must change. There may be reverse causation, common causes or missing information. For example, a difficult period may increase both time spent alone and frequency of a behaviour; isolation may not be the sole cause. The same cue can be relevant in one setting and irrelevant in another. Protect autonomy: modifying another adult's environment, monitoring them or restricting their access without consent can be harmful or inappropriate.

## Framework

**Context–behaviour–impact–options map**

Record a neutral fictional antecedent, reported experience, observable behaviour, immediate consequence and later impact. Add an alternative explanation and a possible voluntary support option. Mark unknowns. Distinguish an ordinary environmental change from an intervention requiring professional guidance. Do not deliberately recreate a trigger or record personal histories.

Text diagram: circumstances → possible cue → behaviour → consequences; dotted conceptual arrows indicate hypotheses rather than established causation.

## Worked example

A fictional adult reports that promotional messages often precede unplanned gambling expenditure. They also report financial pressure and unpredictable working hours. A support map can identify messaging, ease of access and stress as potentially relevant circumstances without deciding which caused the behaviour. They might independently choose to unsubscribe from marketing and contact a gambling support service for further guidance. A peer can offer information if invited. The map does not ask them to gamble again to test causality, share transaction records, or rely on notification changes instead of assessment when harm persists.

### Contrasting case

A person reports checking a stove after an actual safety concern. Advising them to stop safety checks because the stove is a 'trigger' would be unsafe and would misunderstand the behaviour's function. A clinical assessment is needed before selecting treatment for compulsive symptoms.

## Guided practice

**Read the fictional context table.** Case A: an online advert precedes an unwanted spending episode; the person reports regret afterwards. Case B: an agreed recreational session follows dinner and ends as planned. Case C: repeated checking occurs after an intrusive fear and interferes with leaving home. For each, list one antecedent, one known consequence, one alternative explanation and an appropriate boundary.

**Worked solution.** A: advert is a possible cue, not proven cause; financial or other circumstances may matter; offer voluntary support information. B: dinner is an ordinary routine cue, with no reported impairment. C: distress and interference warrant qualified help; do not ask the person to confront the feared situation or stop rituals as a classroom task. Success means separating observed facts from interpretation.

Attempt before consulting the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning gap and retry with an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning and safe boundaries, not the learner's own health or circumstances.

## Independent transfer

Produce a context map for a novel fictional case. Include an environmental influence, an unknown internal experience, two rival explanations and a consent-based support option. Explain why the map cannot determine a clinical intervention.

# MEN-104 / L04 — Withdrawal and referral boundaries

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Evaluate and justify decisions about withdrawal and referral boundaries in the context of compulsive behaviour.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall the difference between physical dependence and addiction. A fictional learner proposes a universal 'quit everything immediately' challenge. Why would that be dangerous for some substances or prescribed medicines?

## Core teaching

Withdrawal refers to effects that may follow reduction or cessation of a substance or medicine after the body has adapted to its presence. Risk depends on the substance, pattern of use, medical context and other factors; a classroom cannot determine individual risk. Physical dependence does not itself prove addiction, and withdrawal is not simply discomfort to overcome through determination. Some withdrawal syndromes can be medically dangerous.

The NHS warns that suddenly stopping alcohol can be dangerous for people who are dependent on it. The FDA likewise warns that abrupt discontinuation or rapid reduction of benzodiazepines after ongoing use can cause serious withdrawal reactions. Decisions about medication changes or stopping potentially dependent substance use require qualified medical guidance. This course deliberately provides no taper schedules, dose calculations, home detox instructions or advice to replace a substance with another. A person seeking help should be directed to a clinician or addiction service for an individual assessment.

Urgent safety takes priority. Severe symptoms, a seizure, collapse, marked confusion or other indications of a medical emergency require prompt emergency help rather than waiting for a routine appointment or attempting self-management. If uncertainty exists about the urgency of symptoms, seek medical advice promptly. Non-substance behavioural difficulties can also cause distress, but the medical risks and care requirements are not interchangeable with alcohol or medication withdrawal. Do not generalise from one category to all others.

## Framework

**Identify risk–refer–protect**

1. Recognise when the question concerns stopping or changing a substance or medicine; do not establish dependence yourself.
2. Refer the question to a qualified medical professional or specialist addiction service before attempting changes.
3. If immediate danger or serious acute symptoms are present, use local emergency services promptly.
4. Offer practical assistance with contacting services if requested, without providing clinical instructions.
5. Record only a fictional referral decision in this course.

This is a safety boundary framework, not a withdrawal risk calculator or medical triage tool.

## Worked example

A fictional adult says they have been using alcohol regularly, are worried they may be dependent and intend to stop abruptly. Their colleague should not prescribe a drinking schedule, give medicines or reassure them that a home detox is safe. A suitable response is to encourage prompt medical advice before making changes and offer to help find local addiction services. If they report a seizure or serious acute symptoms, the priority becomes emergency assistance. A second adult asks how to discontinue a prescribed benzodiazepine; the appropriate contact is the prescribing clinician. Neither example requires the learner to know a dose or estimate a withdrawal threshold.

### Contrasting case

A fictional learner decides to turn off a distracting app notification. This ordinary voluntary change does not justify assuming medically dangerous withdrawal. Equally, it cannot be used as a model for safely stopping alcohol or a prescribed drug.

## Guided practice

**Referral boundary exercise.** A: someone asks a peer for a benzodiazepine taper; B: someone reports possible alcohol dependence and plans to quit immediately; C: someone develops severe acute symptoms after stopping a substance; D: someone wants ordinary help reducing distracting app use. Choose a route, an unsafe response to avoid and a reason.

**Worked solution.** A: prescriber or medical professional; avoid dose schedules. B: prompt medical/addiction advice before changes; avoid unsupervised detox instructions. C: emergency services; do not wait for online assessment. D: voluntary ordinary support is reasonable if there is no disclosed impairment or safety issue; persistent harm may justify professional assessment. The distinction is referral and safety, never a diagnosis.

Attempt before consulting the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning gap and retry with an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning and safe boundaries, not the learner's own health or circumstances.

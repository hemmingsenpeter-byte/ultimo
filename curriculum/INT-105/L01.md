# INT-105 / L01 — Confirmation bias

**Draft — independent review pending.** Outcome: Explain confirmation bias in the context of cognitive biases.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall the difference between an observation and its interpretation. Imagine preferring one of two suppliers before reading their performance reports. Which evidence would you look for first?

## Core teaching

Confirmation bias describes selective handling of information that favours a belief already held. It can occur during search (which reviews you open), interpretation (whether ambiguous results count as success), and recall (which events you remember). These are different mechanisms: an incomplete search is not necessarily deliberate dishonesty. A belief may be correct and still be defended using biased methods. Conversely, choosing evidence relevant to a well-supported hypothesis is not automatically biased. The diagnostic question is whether competing explanations receive comparable evidential scrutiny. A positive test can be informative if an alternative hypothesis predicts a different result. Searching only for instances compatible with a broad claim often cannot distinguish competing accounts. Avoid diagnosing someone's personality from one mistake.

## Framework

Use the symmetric evidence table. Write the current belief, a plausible alternative, observations predicted under each, and a search strategy for evidence that separates them. Specify in advance what would weaken your view. Record evidence on both sides before writing a conclusion. This is an original audit technique, not a proven cure for bias.

## Worked example

A fictional building manager believes contractor A is more reliable because two jobs were completed early. They initially exclude late jobs as unusual. A fair audit compares all jobs during the same period, adjusts for differences in job complexity, and records missed appointments as well as successes. Suppose A completed 18 of 24 on time and B completed 20 of 24. The initial anecdotes no longer justify preferring A; the revised conclusion remains limited by job comparability.

### Contrasting case

A manager checks whether a contractor holds a required qualification. Searching the official register for confirmation is appropriate: the test has a clear success and failure condition. It is not inherently biased to investigate a specific claim.

## Guided practice

Audit the claim that a new booking system reduces errors. Given 8 errors in 100 old bookings and 3 in 50 new bookings, compare rates: 8% versus 6%. Ask whether booking complexity, training or time period differs. Model response: the figures support a tentative difference but do not establish causality. Find a rival explanation and a result that would weaken the claim.

Attempt before reading the model; compare your reasoning and correct a specific gap. Record assumptions and ask what would change your conclusion.

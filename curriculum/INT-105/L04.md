# INT-105 / L04 — Decision journals

**Draft — independent review pending.** Outcome: Evaluate and justify decisions about decision journals in the context of cognitive biases.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall the distinction between evidence before a decision and evidence learned afterwards. Can a well-reasoned choice produce an unfortunate result?

## Core teaching

Outcome bias arises when knowledge of results distorts evaluation of the preceding decision. A decision should be judged against the information, alternatives, probabilities and constraints available when it was made. Outcomes still matter: they provide data for revising assumptions, but they cannot retrospectively become information the decision-maker originally possessed. A decision journal creates a dated record of reasoning so later review can separate process from luck. It is a proposed practical aid, not a guarantee of better decisions. Useful entries include forecasts, opportunity costs, uncertainty and stop or review conditions. Avoid recording only successful decisions or retroactively editing initial estimates. Evaluate both the quality of reasoning and the accuracy of predictions over repeated cases.

## Framework

Use a two-pass journal. Before choosing, record options, relevant evidence, predicted outcomes, probability ranges, reversibility and a review date. After resolution, record actual outcomes, new evidence, original assumption errors and a process improvement. Preserve the original entry. Separate decision quality from outcome quality.

## Worked example

A fictional organiser selects an outdoor venue using a credible forecast, a backup plan and a 20% estimated cancellation risk. Rain forces the backup. A single adverse outcome does not show the initial plan was irrational. If repeated decisions systematically underestimate rain, the forecasts need recalibration. The review may also reveal that the backup was too costly, which is a process issue independent of weather luck.

### Contrasting case

An organiser ignores an explicit severe-weather closure, but the event happens to go ahead safely. The favourable result does not establish that ignoring the constraint was a good decision.

## Guided practice

Write a dated entry choosing between two fictional delivery methods: A has a 10% delay estimate and low cost; B has a 5% delay estimate and higher cost. Identify the consequences and uncertainty. Simulate A being late. Model review: record the delay and investigate probability assumptions, without concluding from one case that B was always preferable.

Attempt before reading the model; compare your reasoning and correct a specific gap. Record assumptions and ask what would change your conclusion.

## Independent transfer

Complete a bias audit containing a confirmation-bias table, anchoring and availability checks, a counterexample and a two-pass journal. Use synthetic cases rather than private decisions.

# INT-105 / L02 — Anchoring and availability

**Draft — independent review pending.** Outcome: Compare and distinguish anchoring and availability in the context of cognitive biases.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the evidence table. Imagine receiving a high opening price for a repair and, separately, hearing about a recent spectacular repair failure. How might these influence judgement?

## Core teaching

Anchoring concerns the influence of an initial value or reference on a later estimate. Availability concerns how easily instances come to mind when judging frequency or plausibility. Neither implies that every reference number or memorable event is misleading. A relevant benchmark can be useful; the issue is whether it is given weight justified by its relevance. Similarly, a vivid event can reveal a real hazard, but vividness does not establish its prevalence. The mechanisms may coexist: an early alarming statistic can be both memorable and numerically anchoring. Separate the two by examining what the decision depends on: a starting number or the accessibility of examples. Estimate rates using defined populations and periods rather than the emotional prominence of an anecdote.

## Framework

Apply two checks: independent estimate before seeing a suggested number; denominator before judging a memorable event. Then compare the new evidence against the estimate and update explicitly. When a benchmark is justified, retain it with a reason; when no reliable frequency exists, say so rather than inventing precision.

## Worked example

A fictional venue requests quotes. The first quote is $12,000; the second is $8,000. The second seems cheap until a scope-matched historical comparison suggests $6,500–$8,500 is plausible. Separately, a colleague recalls one disastrous installation and assumes all installations are risky. The appropriate response is to examine comparable incidents per installation, not to ignore the incident or infer a population rate from it.

### Contrasting case

A technical specification gives a maximum load established by testing. Treating it as a constraint is not an arbitrary anchor. Likewise, an independently documented safety alert may justify action even when the event is uncommon.

## Guided practice

Classify these fictional statements: 'The first estimate was 20 days, so 18 feels fast'; 'I remember two cancelled services, so cancellation is common.' The first suggests anchoring, the second availability. Propose an independent schedule estimate and request the total number of scheduled services and cancellations over a defined period.

Attempt before reading the model; compare your reasoning and correct a specific gap. Record assumptions and ask what would change your conclusion.

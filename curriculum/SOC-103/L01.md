# SOC-103 / L01 — Recurring contact

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Explain recurring contact in the context of friendship and community.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall SOC-101's conversation skills. Two fictional newcomers meet people through different activities: one attends a single large event and one joins a recurring group. Why might opportunities for friendship differ without guaranteeing either person's outcome?

## Core teaching

Friendship is a mutually recognised social relationship, not a status one person can award unilaterally. Acquaintance, activity partner, close friend and community member describe different kinds of connection; there is no requirement to convert every relationship into a close friendship. Recurring contact creates opportunities to become familiar with one another, share experiences and discover compatible interests. It does not by itself establish trust or reciprocal desire for a closer relationship.

Jeffrey Hall's studies examine associations between time spent together, kinds of activity and reported friendship closeness. These results are often reduced to numerical hour targets, but the research is not a universal schedule or causal guarantee. Self-selected relationships and opportunities differ. Spending time beside someone at work may have a different meaning from mutually chosen leisure contact. Do not use a clock to determine whether someone owes friendship.

Community participation also has structural conditions: cost, transport, accessibility, timing, language, caring duties and whether newcomers are welcomed. A recurring setting can be easier to sustain than relying on spontaneous encounters, but the best arrangement is one that is feasible and genuinely wanted. A person may prefer a smaller network, remote contact or activity-based relationships. No minimum number of friends establishes health or social competence. Assess the design of opportunities and respect for choice rather than the learner's real relationship count.

## Framework

**Interest–setting–rhythm–choice**

Identify a shared activity or community purpose. Check whether participation is affordable, accessible, safe and voluntary. Choose a realistic recurring opportunity. Allow interaction to develop without assuming closeness or demanding contact outside the group. Review feasibility and mutual interest; adapt or stop if the arrangement no longer suits. Text model: recurring opportunity → possible shared experiences → mutual choice → possible connection, not guaranteed friendship.

## Worked example

A fictional newcomer enjoys drawing and finds a monthly open sketching session and a weekly evening class. The class offers more frequent contact but conflicts with caring duties; the monthly session is accessible and affordable. They choose the latter and ask how newcomers participate. Over several meetings they share materials and conversation with other members. They do not interpret routine friendliness as a commitment to private friendship. If a member expresses interest in another shared activity, an optional invitation may be appropriate. The success criterion is a sustainable, mutually respectful opportunity, not acquiring a particular number of friends.

### Contrasting case

A person spends forty hours per week in the same office as a colleague. Proximity alone does not establish close friendship or justify asking for private access. Both people may prefer a professional relationship, which is valid.

## Guided practice

**Compare three fictional opportunities.** A: a free weekly group requiring expensive transport. B: a monthly accessible community event. C: a regular online interest group with clear conduct rules. A fictional learner has limited evening availability and prefers written interaction. Identify advantages, barriers and a proportionate trial arrangement.

**Worked solution.** A has frequent contact but may be impractical due to cost and timing. B provides sustainable access but less frequent opportunities. C may fit the preferred communication format, assuming safety and moderation are suitable. Choose a feasible setting rather than maximising contact hours. The learner might trial one option and review whether the activity itself is worthwhile, independently of whether friendships develop.

Calculate the number of ordinary opportunities in a fictional eight-week period if a weekly group meets six times and the learner can attend four. The result is four opportunities, not four units of friendship or a prediction of closeness.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Identify a specific reasoning or communication correction and repeat using an equivalent fictional case. Assess observable task quality, not personality, popularity or another person's compliance.

## Independent transfer

Design a fictional recurring-contact plan with two settings, an access and cost check, realistic availability and a review point. Explain why it creates opportunities rather than guaranteeing friendship.

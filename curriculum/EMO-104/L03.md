# EMO-104 / L03 — Needs versus control

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Apply needs versus control in the context of shame, anger, fear and jealousy.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall EMO-106's distinction between a request, boundary and controlling demand. A fictional person needs predictable arrangements. Does this need establish a right to dictate another adult's unrelated choices?

## Core teaching

A need in this lesson is something important to functioning, safety or participation. A preference is a desired condition; an obligation arises from a legitimate agreement, role or rule. These are contextual distinctions, not a universal needs hierarchy. A proposed solution is not identical to the need. For example, accessible information might be provided through written instructions, audio or another suitable format. Recognising the underlying need expands the available options.

A request proposes an action, an agreement reflects mutual acceptance, and a legitimate rule may create a duty. A self-directed boundary concerns one's own conduct within actual responsibilities. Coercion uses threats, pressure or disproportionate restrictions to undermine another person's autonomy. Describing a demand as a need does not make it legitimate. Conversely, legitimate safety and accessibility requirements do not become optional just because someone describes them as preferences. Consider the actual authority, consent, purpose and consequences.

A constructive approach seeks solutions consistent with both needs and rights. In safe settings, negotiate an option or make an appropriate self-directed decision. A refusal is not necessarily lack of care. Some problems require institutional action rather than a peer's additional effort. Where threats, abuse or coercion are present, direct negotiation may increase risk; appropriate specialist support can be safer. Nobody is responsible for preventing another person's harmful conduct by communicating perfectly. The learning task is to distinguish a legitimate concern from entitlement to control.

## Framework

**Need–options–autonomy–agreement**

1. State the concrete need and the problem supported by evidence.
2. Separate the need from the first preferred solution.
3. Generate at least two options, including one that does not rely on a particular person's compliance.
4. Check authority, existing obligations, accessibility and consent.
5. Communicate a request or a feasible self-directed action, with a safe fallback.

Text model: one need → several possible options → evaluate duties and consent → select a proportionate action. If direct discussion is unsafe, identify a support route instead.

## Worked example

A fictional learner needs quiet to complete an agreed assignment in a shared workspace. Their initial proposal is that everyone must remain silent all day. The need concerns workable conditions; the proposal extends beyond the specific requirement. Alternatives include reserving an available room, agreeing a quiet period or asking the provider for an accessible workspace. An established quiet-use rule might also be relevant; clarify it with the person authorised to administer the space. A request such as 'Could we agree a quiet hour from ten to eleven, or identify another workspace?' permits discussion. If the proposed hour is not feasible, the need remains legitimate and an alternative can be considered.

### Contrasting case

A fictional partner says reassurance requires access to another person's passwords. The reported need does not establish authority to monitor private communications. A voluntary discussion or qualified support may be appropriate, but compliance cannot be treated as proof of love or trust.

## Guided practice

**Separate three needs from proposed solutions.** A: a volunteer needs clear availability expectations. B: a student needs accessible course materials. C: a friend seeks reassurance after an ambiguous message. For each, list two options and identify relevant authority.

**Worked solution.** A: clarify the agreed schedule and the designated urgent channel; if needed, negotiate an authorised change. B: request an accessible format or a suitable substitute from the provider. C: ask for clarification if safe and wanted, or choose another feasible support option without pressuring the person. The first two may involve genuine role or organisational obligations. The third does not justify surveillance. If an option is declined, identify what remains feasible.

Now compare the voluntary request in C with a mandatory safety instruction in A. Explain why the former allows refusal while the latter may reflect an actual duty. Avoid treating every request as interchangeable.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Identify a specific reasoning correction and repeat with a novel fictional case. Assess evidence, appropriateness and reasoning rather than personal emotional states.

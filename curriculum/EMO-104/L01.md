The goal is to distinguish emotions and select justified corrective actions without assessing anyone's mental health.

## Core teaching

Shame and guilt are often studied as self-conscious emotions: they concern evaluations of oneself or one's conduct against standards and expectations. An influential distinction concerns the scope of the evaluation. Shame tends to focus on the person as a whole; guilt tends to focus on a specific action or omission. Real experiences overlap and the words are used differently across settings. Embarrassment, regret and fear of consequences can also be relevant. These concepts describe possible experiences rather than a reliable method of inferring another person's feelings.

A specific action can be investigated and corrected. A global negative evaluation may obscure which part of the situation is actually changeable. But shame does not inevitably produce withdrawal, and guilt does not guarantee repair. People can feel guilty about events they did not cause, or cause harm without feeling guilty. The presence or absence of an emotion does not establish responsibility. Evidence about duties, actual conduct, impact and reasonable influence is necessary.

Accountability asks what happened, what was within one's role, who was affected and what correction is feasible. It differs from self-condemnation, humiliation or punishment. When systems contribute to a failure, a good review considers both personal actions and the system conditions. The affected party may need a practical remedy rather than a display of distress. Persistent emotional difficulty may warrant professional support; learners are not asked to recount personal experiences or judge whether an emotion is clinically appropriate.

## Framework

**Action–impact–responsibility–repair**

1. Describe an observable action or omission without a character judgement.
2. Establish the applicable obligation and concrete impact.
3. Identify what was within the actor's reasonable control and what was not.
4. Select a proportionate correction and prevention step.
5. Identify evidence that would change the judgement; review the result.

Text model: specific action → evidence of impact → justified responsibility → appropriate correction. This is a teaching map, not a diagnostic instrument. If responsibility remains disputed, investigate before accepting or attributing blame.

## Worked example

A fictional analyst sends a report with an incorrect total. A colleague identifies the error before the report is used. The analyst initially says, 'I cannot be trusted with anything.' That conclusion goes beyond the available evidence. A narrower description is that a formula omitted one row and the verification step did not catch it. The analyst can correct the formula, tell the appropriate recipient what changed and add a separate check of the total. If the shared spreadsheet template also had a defect, notify its owner. The error is not excused, but the corrective action is connected to actual responsibility. A later review checks whether the corrected formula covers every row rather than measuring how ashamed the analyst appears.

### Contrasting case

An employee feels guilty because a colleague was disappointed when an optional invitation was declined. The colleague's disappointment does not establish that an obligation was breached. Conversely, someone who feels no guilt may still owe a correction for a documented error.

## Guided practice

**Classify the fictional statements.** A: 'I missed the agreed handover.' B: 'I ruin everything.' C: 'I feel responsible for the weather delay.' Identify the scope of evaluation, evidence required and a proportionate response.

**Worked solution.** A describes an action; establish the agreed duty, impact and correction. B is a global judgement; narrow it to a verifiable event, then identify a prevention step. C reports a feeling of responsibility but does not establish control of weather; check whether preparation duties were actually assigned. If the actor failed to communicate a known delay, that narrower breach may be actionable. A strong response neither excuses harm nor treats distress as proof of blame.

Now compare two possible remedies for A: sending an overdue handover immediately versus arranging a replacement through the authorised coordinator. Which is suitable depends on whether the original handover remains useful and whether the person has authority. Record that condition explicitly.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Identify a specific reasoning correction and repeat with a novel fictional case. Assess evidence, appropriateness and reasoning rather than personal emotional states.

## Independent transfer

Create a fictional accountability map for a missed commitment. Include one legitimate responsibility, one factor outside control, a correction and prevention step, and one rival explanation. Revise the map if later evidence shows that no breach occurred.

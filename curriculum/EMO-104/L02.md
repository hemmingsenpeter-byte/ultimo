# EMO-104 / L02 — Anger fear and jealousy

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Compare and distinguish anger fear and jealousy in the context of shame, anger, fear and jealousy.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the event–appraisal–response map. A fictional employee misses an opportunity and worries about losing an important relationship. Which emotional experiences might coexist? Explain why naming the emotion cannot establish the facts.

## Core teaching

Anger commonly concerns a perceived obstruction, offence or injustice. Fear commonly concerns a perceived threat or danger. Jealousy often concerns a perceived threat to a valued relationship involving another person; envy can concern wanting an advantage or possession another person has. These distinctions are useful working concepts, not rigid definitions. Ordinary words overlap, multiple emotions can occur at once, and the reported experience depends on context and interpretation.

An action tendency is an inclination, not a command. Anger may accompany an urge to challenge a decision; fear may accompany protective action; jealousy may accompany a desire for reassurance or information. These examples do not establish universal mechanisms or justify every possible action. An emotion may reflect a real issue, but the facts must be examined separately. Jealousy does not establish betrayal or authorise monitoring another person's communications, restricting friendships or demanding account access. Fear may reflect a genuine hazard, which must not be dismissed as a mistaken interpretation.

Evaluate a response using the actual circumstances, evidence, safety, legitimate goals and respect for autonomy. If immediate danger exists, appropriate emergency action takes priority over a conversation. In a safe but ambiguous situation, clarification may be useful. A documented unfair decision may require a formal review rather than reinterpretation. The aim is not to suppress difficult feelings or tolerate harm; it is to distinguish emotion, appraisal and conduct. No clinical conclusion can be drawn from these cases.

## Framework

**Emotion–appraisal–evidence–action matrix**

Write the reported emotion (or leave it unknown), the apparent goal or threat, observable facts, missing information and two possible actions. Check whether either action violates privacy, autonomy or legitimate duties. Prioritise immediate safety where relevant. Choose an appropriate response under uncertainty and define what new information would change it. Compare the actions rather than ranking the emotions.

Text model: feeling ≠ evidence of motive; action must meet safety, evidence and autonomy criteria.

## Worked example

A fictional team member learns a colleague was chosen for a desirable assignment. They report anger about the selection process, fear about their future opportunities, envy of the assignment and concern that a mentor is becoming less available. The facts establish that the assignment was made and the learner was not selected. They do not establish the criteria, future opportunities or mentor's motives. A constructive response requests the selection criteria and feedback through the appropriate channel. If an actual procedural breach emerges, the learner can seek a review. Demanding that the colleague withdraw or the mentor stop helping others would not be justified by emotional intensity.

### Contrasting case

A person receives a credible threat. Treating their fear as an interpretation to be positively reframed could be unsafe. Follow applicable safety procedures and seek appropriate assistance. The classroom matrix is not an individual danger assessment.

## Guided practice

**Compare three fictional cases.** A: an agreed commitment is documented and broken. B: an unfamiliar sound causes concern, but its source is unknown. C: a fictional partner spends time with a friend, with no established breach. For each identify a possible appraisal, one unknown and two responses.

**Worked solution.** A: perceived injustice may accompany anger; establish impact and seek accountability rather than retaliation. B: assess the circumstances safely and follow appropriate procedures if there is actual danger; do not assume either safety or danger from the feeling alone. C: jealousy or insecurity may be reported but does not establish betrayal; discuss agreed expectations if safe and wanted, without surveillance. In each case explain which fact would justify a different action.

A second learner argues that the person in C must obtain reassurance immediately. Critique that proposal: reassurance may be requested, not demanded, and other people's privacy and capacity remain relevant.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Identify a specific reasoning correction and repeat with a novel fictional case. Assess evidence, appropriateness and reasoning rather than personal emotional states.

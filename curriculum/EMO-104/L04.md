## Orientation and retrieval

A fictional person apologises for missing a deadline but repeats the behaviour next week. Retrieve the accountability map and identify what evidence would show repair beyond the apology.

## Core teaching

Repair describes actions addressing a breach, harm or disruption in a shared activity or relationship. An apology is one possible component. Other remedies may involve correcting inaccurate information, restoring a service, replacing a damaged item, changing a process or agreeing a more feasible arrangement. Lewicki and colleagues studied different apology components in trust-violation scenarios. Their results concern perceived effectiveness under studied conditions, not a universal method that guarantees reconciliation.

A useful apology can acknowledge a specific act, recognise its impact, accept responsibility justified by evidence and propose an appropriate correction. An explanation may clarify contributing circumstances, but it should not erase the impact or shift responsibility without evidence. Where facts remain disputed, acknowledge known consequences and investigate fairly rather than inventing admissions. A promise to change must be realistic and capable of review. Follow-through means evidence that the corrective commitment occurred; a repeated apology without changed conduct may leave the underlying problem unresolved.

Repair preserves autonomy. The affected person can decline contact, reject a proposed remedy or decide not to resume a relationship. Forgiveness is not owed. Correcting an error and rebuilding trust are different outcomes; the latter may require time or may not happen. Serious misconduct can require formal processes or specialist support rather than informal discussion. In unsafe circumstances, do not insist on confrontation or joint repair. The lesson assesses the proposed action, not the affected person's reaction.

## Framework

**Acknowledge–impact–responsibility–remedy–review**

State the known act and effect. Accept the responsibility supported by evidence. Identify one feasible remedy and a prevention step; check authority and resources. Ask whether an additional appropriate action is needed if contact is wanted and safe. Review observable commitments. Respect a refusal to engage, and use formal or specialist routes when the problem exceeds your role. This is an original teaching checklist, not a guarantee of restored trust.

## Worked example

A fictional organiser distributes an incorrect event time, causing participants to arrive before the venue opens. They say: 'I sent the wrong time in yesterday's notice. That caused people to arrive before the room was available. I've corrected the notice and will check future times against the booking before sending them.' The organiser also asks the responsible coordinator whether a practical remedy for participants is appropriate; they do not promise reimbursement without authority. If someone does not want a further conversation, the correction can proceed without demanding a response. The next notice and verification record supply evidence of prevention, unlike a statement of good intentions alone.

### Contrasting case

Someone apologises repeatedly for insulting remarks, continues the remarks and demands forgiveness. The apology does not itself remedy the conduct or entitle them to renewed contact. Formal or specialist support may be appropriate.

## Guided practice

**Compare responses to a fictional missed file deadline.** A: 'Sorry you were upset, but you should have reminded me.' B: 'I missed our deadline. I can send the file at the revised time if useful, and will confirm future deadlines in the shared schedule.'

**Worked solution.** A shifts responsibility and identifies no remedy. B names the breach and proposes an observable correction, but should check whether another consequence requires action and whether the new timing is feasible. If the original access was unavailable, clarify that contributing fact rather than making an unsupported claim about blame. If the recipient does not want contact, use an authorised correction channel. A strong response specifies the review condition: the file is actually received and later deadlines are recorded and met.

Compare the immediate corrective action (send the file) with the preventive action (confirm future deadlines). Neither guarantees future trust, but both can be evaluated.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Identify a specific reasoning correction and repeat with a novel fictional case. Assess evidence, appropriateness and reasoning rather than personal emotional states.

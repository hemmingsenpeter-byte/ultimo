# MEN-107 / L01 — Pressure and attention

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Explain pressure and attention in the context of performance under pressure.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall working memory and task switching from MEN-102. A fictional presenter knows the material in rehearsal but loses their place when asked an unexpected question. What task demands changed, and what other explanations are possible?

## Core teaching

Pressure refers here to circumstances in which performance is perceived to carry important consequences or evaluation. It is not identical to anxiety, physiological arousal or actual danger. People may experience the same circumstance differently. Performance can improve, remain similar or deteriorate under pressure, depending on the skill, environment, preparation and individual. Avoid the simplistic claim that all people share one ideal arousal level or that feeling nervous inevitably predicts poor performance.

Two research accounts of pressure-related skill failure distinguish distraction and explicit monitoring. In a distraction account, attention required for the task is diverted to concerns or unrelated demands. In an explicit monitoring account, trying to consciously control a well-practised procedure can disrupt its fluent execution. DeCaro and colleagues found that different experimental tasks and pressure manipulations could reveal different patterns. Neither model diagnoses a person or establishes that one technique works across all skills. A novice may need explicit step-by-step instruction, whereas a familiar task may benefit from a simple task-relevant cue.

Separately investigate preparation, ambiguity, interruptions, accessibility, task difficulty and genuine resource gaps. Pressure may reveal an incomplete skill rather than being the sole explanation. A sensible teaching response reduces unnecessary demands and improves access to task cues while maintaining safety. Do not deliberately manufacture intense distress or humiliation to test performance; low-stakes voluntary rehearsal is the focus of the next lesson.

## Framework

**Task–pressure–attention–support map**

Describe the skill and an observable quality criterion. Identify what changed between conditions. Propose at least two explanations for any error, distinguishing distraction, excessive monitoring and ordinary preparation or access issues. Choose a low-risk support suited to the task, such as a cue card or clearer question. Compare with a novel equivalent task rather than drawing conclusions from a single result.

## Worked example

A fictional presenter can explain three cost comparisons with notes. During a meeting they are asked a new question while trying to remember the next slide, and lose the distinction between total cost and annual cost. Possible causes include competing working-memory demands, uncertainty about the new question and insufficient preparation for unit comparisons. A cue card listing units and three key messages may help. Rehearsing a comparable question in a voluntary setting can test whether the support is useful. One mistake does not establish a pressure disorder or inability to present.

### Contrasting case

A novice learning a new calculation may need to consciously check each step. Advising them to 'stop thinking and trust instinct' could increase mistakes. Conversely, demanding elaborate conscious monitoring of an already fluent, low-risk task may impose unnecessary demands.

## Guided practice

**Compare three fictional failures.** A: a learner forgets a well-understood number while worrying about evaluation. B: an experienced performer repeatedly restarts a familiar phrase while scrutinising each word. C: a beginner has never learned the required concept. Identify plausible mechanisms, alternatives and appropriate rehearsal supports.

**Worked solution.** A: competing attention is plausible; an accessible reference cue may help. B: excessive self-monitoring is plausible but not established; focus on meaning and use a simple cue in a low-stakes practice. C: instruction and feedback are needed. Do not infer diagnoses or use high-pressure testing. In each case, identify a quality measure such as correct units or accurate explanation.

Attempt before consulting the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning gap and retry with an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning and safe boundaries, not the learner's own health or circumstances.

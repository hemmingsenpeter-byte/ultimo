## Core teaching

A debrief or after-action review is a structured examination of an experience intended to support learning and future performance. It differs from a verdict, venting session or clinical discussion of traumatic experiences. A useful debrief begins with the intended objective and observable facts, compares them with the result, explores plausible explanations, identifies what worked and selects a specific next action. Explanations should remain tentative when evidence is limited. Feedback focuses on behaviours and reasoning rather than personal character.

Meta-analyses of individual and team debriefs report beneficial average effects in studied performance and training settings. Implementation and context influence the results; a debrief format is not a guarantee of a fixed improvement. Psychological safety and consent matter: learners should be able to ask questions, disagree with interpretations and decline an unnecessary recording. An observer's feedback may itself be incomplete or mistaken. Separate the observation ('the annual cost was omitted') from interpretation ('the speaker was unprepared').

A review becomes useful when it produces a testable next attempt. Correct a material error first, practise a novel equivalent task and compare against the same criterion. Avoid an endless search for personal faults or rehashing distress. A classroom debrief after an ordinary voluntary rehearsal is not psychological debriefing after trauma, clinical counselling, disciplinary investigation or an incident review in a regulated setting. Serious incidents require appropriate formal procedures and qualified support.

## Framework

**Intention–facts–gap–options–test**

1. What was the objective and criterion?
2. What happened, based on observable evidence?
3. What matched the objective, and what differed?
4. Which explanations are plausible, and what remains unknown?
5. What one or two changes should be tested next, with an equivalent case and review criterion?

Document the learner's interpretation and any disagreement respectfully. Stop if the activity is no longer useful or comfortable.

## Worked example

A fictional presenter intends to explain two options accurately and answer one question. The observer records that the learner explained both options, stated the correct totals, but confused the units in a follow-up answer. The learner says the question was ambiguous. The debrief distinguishes the observation from possible causes: unclear wording, missing unit cue or a skill gap. The next attempt uses a new comparison, an explicit unit label and permission to ask for clarification. Success means an accurate, qualified response, not a more confident appearance.

### Contrasting case

A review that says 'you are not good under pressure' offers no specific evidence or practice direction and may unfairly label the learner. A review that concentrates only on praise also misses correctable errors. Respectful specificity is more useful than either.

## Guided practice

**Write an after-action review.** Fictional objective: compare two suppliers, identify the cheaper total cost and explain a limitation. Observation: the learner correctly calculates total costs, overlooks an accessibility requirement and answers a question accurately after asking for clarification. Write one strength, one gap, two rival explanations and a next attempt.

**Worked solution.** Strength: accurate calculation and clarification. Gap: omission of accessibility criterion. Explanations: requirement not prominent in the brief or insufficient checking of decision constraints. Next attempt: use a new supplier comparison and a checklist containing cost, access and uncertainty; verify the requirement before concluding. Ask the learner whether feedback matches their experience. Do not turn the review into a personal assessment.

Attempt before consulting the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning gap and retry with an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning and safe boundaries, not the learner's own health or circumstances.

## Knowledge check

1. What distinguishes observation from interpretation?
2. Why include strengths as well as gaps?
3. Do debrief studies guarantee a fixed improvement?
4. What should follow a useful debrief?

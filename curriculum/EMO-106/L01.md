# EMO-106 / L01 — Boundaries versus control

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Explain boundaries versus control in the context of boundaries and assertiveness.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall the distinction between a request and a constructive response. A fictional friend says 'My boundary is that you must never disagree with me.' What is the difference between identifying one's own limits and controlling someone else's choices?

## Core teaching

A boundary is used here as a practical description of limits on one's own participation, access, availability or conduct, together with decisions about how to respond when those limits are reached. It is not a single universally standardised psychological construct. A request asks another person to consider a change; an agreement is a mutually accepted commitment; a rule may be legitimate within a particular role or institution; coercion attempts to restrict another person's autonomy through pressure, threats or control. These categories can overlap in ordinary language, so evaluate what the action does rather than accepting its label.

A self-directed boundary might be 'I am not available for routine messages after our agreed hours; I will reply the next working day.' It governs availability and response. 'You must never contact anyone else after hours' restricts another person's choices without an obvious legitimate basis. A mutually agreed on-call arrangement could legitimately specify who handles urgent requests. The authority, consent, reason, proportionality and effect of the arrangement matter. A statement using the word 'I' is not automatically respectful; it can still be used as a threat. Similarly, legitimate safety rules and safeguarding obligations can limit conduct without being arbitrary control.

Boundaries are not a licence to ignore existing duties, discriminate, punish others or force compliance. They can be revised through consent and appropriate processes; another person may choose not to accept a proposed arrangement. Power differences matter: a manager's request may feel less optional than a peer's request. In coercive or abusive situations, asserting a boundary may be unsafe and a person is not responsible for another's harmful conduct. Specialist support may be preferable to direct discussion. This course teaches decision and communication literacy using fictional low-risk cases.

## Framework

**Limit–authority–autonomy–action test**

Identify the limit and whose action it concerns. Ask whether the proposed restriction has legitimate authority or mutual consent, and whether it is proportionate to the actual issue. Distinguish a request from an agreed obligation. Specify an action within the person's control and compatible with existing duties. Check safety, privacy and power before communicating.

Text model: own limit → communicated request or decision → other person's choice → appropriate own response. Do not equate the other person's compliance with competence.

## Worked example

A fictional volunteer is asked to handle routine messages every evening despite agreeing to weekend-only availability. They clarify their existing agreement: 'I'm available on Saturday for routine scheduling; please direct urgent safeguarding matters through the designated channel.' This states a limit and preserves essential obligations. They may ask the coordinator to confirm the arrangement or discuss changes. A contrasting demand would be 'You are not allowed to ask any other volunteer for help.' That limits others' choices without justification. If the coordinator has legitimate authority to revise a role, the volunteer can discuss the changed expectations and decide whether participation remains feasible.

### Contrasting case

An authorised supervisor instructs workers not to enter a restricted hazardous area. The restriction concerns a legitimate safety duty, not necessarily improper interpersonal control. Context, authority and proportionality distinguish it from a personal attempt to dictate unrelated choices.

## Guided practice

**Classify four fictional statements.** A: 'I will end this optional discussion if insults continue.' B: 'You cannot speak to your friends unless I approve.' C: 'We agreed each person will cover one weekend shift.' D: 'This laboratory requires authorised access.' Identify category, authority and any safety issue.

**Worked solution.** A is a potential participation boundary if ending the discussion is safe and feasible. B is controlling and may indicate coercive conduct; do not recommend confrontation as a default. C is an agreement subject to its terms and legitimate changes. D is a potentially legitimate institutional safety rule. Evaluate actions, not labels. A well-formed answer notes that even self-directed statements can be coercive depending on their use.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify one specific correction and apply it to an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning, evidence, safe boundaries and task quality rather than personal characteristics or emotional states.

## Independent transfer

Create three fictional boundary statements, one mutual agreement and one legitimate role rule. Apply the test to each and revise an ambiguous statement into a non-coercive request or self-directed action. Explain a context where communication could be unsafe.

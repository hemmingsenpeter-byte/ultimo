# EMO-106 / L03 — Declining

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Apply declining in the context of boundaries and assertiveness.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall the difference between an optional favour and an agreed duty. A fictional learner is invited to take on an additional task that exceeds their available capacity. How can they decline without making an unsupported excuse or insulting the requester?

## Core teaching

Declining means communicating that one will not accept a request or offer. For a genuinely optional request, a clear refusal may be sufficient. A person need not disclose sensitive health, financial or family details to justify every decision. However, commitments and role responsibilities can create legitimate obligations: declining an assigned safety-critical responsibility or an existing agreement may require authorised alternatives, handover or negotiation. Assess what is actually being requested before choosing wording.

An effective refusal can be brief, specific and respectful. It may acknowledge the request, state the decision and optionally offer an alternative if one is feasible and genuinely wanted. Alternatives are not mandatory. Avoid promising future availability just to soften a refusal if that promise is unlikely to be kept. Distinguish a firm refusal from a counterproposal ('I cannot take the full task, but I can check the summary tomorrow') and from a request for time to decide. If the requester repeats an optional request, restating the decision may be appropriate in a safe context.

Declining is context-sensitive. Cultural norms may favour different degrees of directness; accessibility and power can affect how refusal is received. An individual may choose an intermediary, written response or organisational route rather than face-to-face refusal. In coercive relationships, direct refusal may increase risk and should not be prescribed as proof of strength. Specialist advice may be needed. CCI's assertiveness resources include declining requests; these fictional examples are original teaching practice, not treatment or a rule that one must always say no.

## Framework

**Classify–decide–communicate–handover**

Classify the request as optional, negotiable, agreed or required; identify safety and authority. Decide what is feasible. Communicate a clear refusal, counterproposal or request for decision time, without unnecessary disclosure. If an existing duty is affected, arrange an authorised alternative or handover. Identify whether further discussion is safe and useful.

Text model: request → obligation check → feasible decision → communication → review or appropriate handover.

## Worked example

A fictional learner is asked to prepare an optional extra presentation tomorrow. They have already committed to another task. They respond: 'Thanks for asking. I can't take on the extra presentation tomorrow.' If they want to offer an alternative, they might say 'I can share the existing public summary by Thursday, if useful.' The alternative is optional and only offered if feasible. If the presentation was actually assigned under an agreed role, they would instead raise the capacity conflict and ask the responsible manager to prioritise or authorise reassignment. The same wording is not suitable for every obligation.

### Contrasting case

A worker is responsible for a necessary handover before ending a shift. Simply saying 'my boundary is that I leave now' may breach a legitimate safety requirement. The appropriate response is to obtain an authorised handover or follow the relevant safety escalation procedure, not silently abandon the duty.

## Guided practice

**Choose a refusal type.** A: optional social invitation. B: requested extra work with a competing deadline. C: agreed duty that cannot be completed because required access is unavailable. D: a repeated optional request in a safe context. Write a response and classify it.

**Worked solution.** A: 'Thanks for the invitation; I won't be able to join.' B: identify the conflict and ask the authorised decision-maker to prioritise, rather than assuming the work is optional. C: report the missing access and seek an authorised alternative. D: restate the refusal clearly without escalating or inventing a reason. A learner need not provide a personal explanation. If any context becomes unsafe, seek appropriate support.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify one specific correction and apply it to an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning, evidence, safe boundaries and task quality rather than personal characteristics or emotional states.

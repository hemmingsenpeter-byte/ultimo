# EMO-106 / L02 — Assertive requests

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the limit–authority–autonomy test. A fictional colleague needs another person to provide figures by Tuesday. What distinguishes a clear assertive request from an indirect hint or coercive demand?

## Core teaching

Assertiveness is commonly taught as direct, respectful expression of one's needs, views or limits while recognising others' rights. It differs from passivity, in which a person's legitimate needs may remain unexpressed, and aggression, in which expression may disregard others' rights or dignity. These are descriptions of communication patterns, not personality categories. A quiet or indirect communication style can be appropriate within a cultural setting or an unsafe context. Assertiveness is not measured by volume, eye contact, speed or the amount of personal disclosure.

A useful request states the desired action, relevant context, timing and a feasible way to respond. The request should be specific enough to understand but not more intrusive than necessary. Separate facts from accusations; explain practical impact without pretending the other person's motives are known. Allow refusal or negotiation when the issue is genuinely optional. Where a duty or authorised instruction is involved, identify the requirement and relevant escalation process transparently instead of framing a non-optional obligation as a free choice.

Communication preferences, accessibility and power differences matter. Written requests may provide processing time or a clear record; an oral discussion may allow clarification; another agreed format may work better. The Centre for Clinical Interventions provides general assertiveness resources, but the classroom scripts here are original examples, not clinical treatment or guarantees of compliance. A well-formed request can still be declined. Evaluate the clarity and fairness of the communication rather than whether it achieves the desired outcome. If direct communication creates a safety risk, seek appropriate support.

## Framework

**Fact–need–specific request–response option**

State relevant facts. Explain the concrete need or goal. Request an observable action and a realistic time. Identify whether it is optional, agreed or required by a legitimate role; offer a response or negotiation route as appropriate. Check safety, accessibility and whether the other person has authority to agree.

Example structure: 'The report is due Wednesday. I need the figures to verify totals. Could you send the agreed table by Tuesday noon, or let me know if that is not feasible?'

## Worked example

A fictional team member needs access to a shared file to complete an authorised task. An indirect hint, 'It would be nice if people shared things', does not identify the file or deadline. An aggressive statement, 'Send it now or you're useless', attacks the person and ignores authority. A clearer request is: 'I need the current comparison table for the agreed report by Wednesday. Could you grant the appropriate access by Tuesday, or tell me who can authorise it?' The other person may not have permission to grant access; the alternative route acknowledges this. If the information is sensitive, use the authorised process and do not request credentials.

### Contrasting case

A mandatory safeguarding action is not a negotiable favour. Communicating the applicable responsibility clearly and escalating through the proper channel may be necessary. Assertiveness does not mean treating all duties as optional.

## Guided practice

**Rewrite three requests.** A: 'You never help me.' B: 'Drop everything and fix this.' C: 'I guess I can manage without the information.' Use fictional circumstances: a shared report requires a table by Friday, the colleague has other agreed responsibilities, and the table may need authorisation.

**Worked solution.** A: 'I need the table to finish our report. Can you provide it by Thursday or identify the authorised contact?' B: clarify the deadline and ask what is feasible, without overriding unrelated duties. C: explicitly state the missing dependency rather than silently accepting a preventable risk. Each request describes the task, timing and response option. Do not claim that a particular wording guarantees cooperation.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify one specific correction and apply it to an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning, evidence, safe boundaries and task quality rather than personal characteristics or emotional states.

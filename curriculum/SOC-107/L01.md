# SOC-107 / L01 — Contextual etiquette

## Orientation and retrieval

## Core teaching

Etiquette refers to conventions governing social conduct in particular settings. A convention can reduce uncertainty by establishing expectations about arrival, introductions, turn-taking, dress, communication or shared spaces. It is different from an ethical duty, which concerns a more fundamental responsibility, and from an authorised requirement, which may be set by an institution. These categories sometimes overlap. A customary greeting is not equivalent to a safety rule. A published check-in procedure may be necessary even if a customary greeting is optional.

Contextual etiquette involves identifying the purpose and participants of an event before selecting appropriate conduct. A formal discussion may require agenda-based contributions; a relaxed gathering may allow spontaneous exchanges. Neither style is inherently superior. Conventions differ across communities and situations, including within the same community. Avoid inferring preferences from someone's name, appearance or nationality. A newcomer can ask the organiser for guidance rather than memorising supposed universal rules.

Distinguish consideration from conformity. Arriving at the agreed time, informing a host of a delay and respecting shared resources can make arrangements predictable. However, an etiquette convention should not override access, autonomy, dignity or legitimate safety obligations. Someone may decline physical contact or prefer another communication format. Where unfamiliar local cultural practices matter, seek appropriate guidance and respect individual choice. The learner is assessed on contextual reasoning and practical consideration, not knowledge of elite customs or imitation of one social style.

## Framework

**Purpose–expectation–choice–impact**

Identify the setting and its purpose. Separate explicit requirements, informal conventions and individual preferences. Verify important unknowns with the host or relevant authority. Choose a feasible action that respects others' time, access and autonomy. If a convention conflicts with safety or dignity, seek an appropriate alternative. Review the impact rather than judging someone's character.

Text model: actual setting → verified expectation → considerate action; customary behaviour is not proof of obligation.

## Worked example

A fictional learner attends a committee meeting at 9 am and an informal gathering later. For the meeting, the organiser has requested advance registration, written materials and contributions through the chair. The learner checks in, reads the material and uses the agreed speaking route. At the gathering, guests can arrive within a stated hour and join activities voluntarily. The learner does not impose the meeting's speaking order on the gathering. In both settings, they respect refusal and communicate changes. If unable to attend at the agreed time, they notify the relevant contact. The conduct is appropriate because it fits actual arrangements, not because it conforms to a single universal etiquette code.

### Contrasting case

A fictional organiser expects all participants to shake hands. A guest declines and offers a verbal greeting. Respecting the guest's choice preserves consideration without compulsory physical contact. The convention is not a valid reason to pressure them.

## Guided practice

**Classify five fictional expectations.** A: check in at a secure building. B: use the chair's speaking procedure. C: exchange handshakes. D: wear specified protective equipment in a hazardous area. E: notify a host if an agreed booking must be cancelled. Identify the likely function, authority and appropriate alternative.

**Worked solution.** A and D may involve genuine security or safety requirements and need authorised compliance. B is an agreed coordination procedure, which may need accessible alternatives. C is a possible greeting convention, not a requirement for unwanted contact. E is a considerate action connected to an actual arrangement. Verify context instead of relying on labels.

Now consider a fictional participant who cannot use the usual check-in technology. Ask the organiser for an authorised accessible route rather than assuming they should bypass security. The solution preserves both access and the legitimate requirement.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify a specific correction and repeat with an equivalent fictional case. Explain why the action fits the actual context and what would change the decision.

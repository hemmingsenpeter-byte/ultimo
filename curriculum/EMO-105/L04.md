# EMO-105 / L04 — Responsive communication

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Evaluate and justify decisions about responsive communication in the context of attachment and development.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the attachment-function and label maps. A fictional friend asks for help after a difficult meeting. What makes communication responsive without requiring the friend to solve every problem?

## Core teaching

Responsiveness in this lesson involves noticing what a person communicates, checking its meaning and responding appropriately within one's capacity and role. It is not constant availability, agreement with every assertion or automatic fulfilment of every request. Research on adult close relationships examines how attachment-related orientations interact with stress and support, but does not prescribe one script for all people. The educational aim is to improve observable communication rather than change a psychological score.

A response may acknowledge what was heard, ask what kind of help is wanted, offer a feasible action or communicate a clear limit. Predictability can come from realistic commitments and follow-through rather than promises of unrestricted access. A person may decline help or prefer another source of support. Caregiving responsibilities differ from adult peer relationships: developmental needs, role and safeguarding duties matter. A generic dialogue exercise cannot establish parenting competence or clinical skill.

Misunderstanding can be corrected without declaring a relationship insecure. A person may want information rather than reassurance, practical assistance rather than advice, or no immediate conversation. If a request exceeds one's capacity, identify appropriate alternative support if wanted. Persistent impairment may warrant qualified care; credible danger requires an appropriate safety response. Do not insist that a person rely on a single partner, peer or tutor. Respect autonomy and relevant duties rather than treating cooperation as a measure of attachment security.

## Framework

**Notice–check–respond–confirm**

Notice the actual request or reported experience. Check what kind of support is wanted with a tentative question. Offer feasible help or communicate a realistic limit. Confirm any agreed action and check it later if appropriate. Respect refusal and preserve relevant safety duties. Refer clinical or safety concerns to suitable support. No psychological outcome is promised.

## Worked example

A fictional colleague says, 'I'm worried about tomorrow's meeting.' A responsive reply might ask whether the agenda is unclear or whether the person would prefer to talk. They explain that the agenda is missing. The colleague helps find the authorised document and agrees to check one question later. They cannot offer evening availability and say so rather than promise constant contact. The assistance matches the stated need without assigning an attachment label. If the colleague declines further assistance, respect that choice unless legitimate duties require another action.

### Contrasting case

A fictional friend demands that another person remain available at all times and provide private message access to prove care. Responsiveness does not require relinquishing privacy, abandoning obligations or agreeing to coercive requests. Realistic availability and alternative support are appropriate in safe circumstances.

## Guided practice

**Write two fictional exchanges.** A: a person wants practical help with instructions. B: a person wants to be heard rather than advised. Include a checking question, a bounded offer and a response to refusal.

**Worked solution.** A: 'Would reviewing the instructions together help? I can do that for ten minutes now.' B: 'It sounds as though the meeting was difficult. Would you prefer me to listen rather than offer suggestions?' If the speaker corrects the interpretation, acknowledge the correction. If either offer is refused, do not insist. Confirm actual commitments only when both people agree.

Now suppose A requires clinical expertise rather than ordinary instruction. The peer should communicate their limit and offer help identifying qualified support if wanted, not claim expertise. Explain why the revised response is still responsive.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Identify a specific reasoning correction and repeat with a novel fictional case. Assess evidence, appropriateness and reasoning rather than personal emotional states.

# MEN-106 / L01 — Acute and chronic stress

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Explain acute and chronic stress in the context of stress management.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall from EMO-101 that an emotion and an interpretation are distinct. Consider a fictional learner facing a sudden deadline versus a learner with repeated unresolved demands for months. What differs in duration, recovery opportunity and consequences?

## Core teaching

Stress is a response to challenging or threatening circumstances, involving psychological and bodily processes. Short-term stress can accompany preparation or mobilisation in a demanding situation. Acute refers here to a relatively short-lived response; chronic refers to demands or stress responses that persist or recur over a longer period, often with limited recovery. These terms describe patterns, not a diagnosis or a fixed threshold. People respond differently to similar demands, and an apparently minor event may be experienced as substantial.

Stress can affect sleep, attention, emotions and ordinary functioning. The presence of a stress response does not establish a particular medical or psychological condition. Physical symptoms may have multiple causes, including conditions requiring medical attention. Do not interpret chest pain, breathing problems or other serious symptoms as merely stress. The appropriate action depends on urgency and context. Repeated difficult circumstances may require practical and organisational change, not just a technique for managing feelings.

Distinguish an external stressor, a person's appraisal of it, the response and the resources available. Appraisal means how a situation is understood relative to demands, meaning and perceived capacity; it is not a claim that stress is imagined or self-inflicted. An impossible workload remains a problem regardless of a person's mindset. The instructional model below helps identify patterns without requiring learners to disclose traumatic or private experiences.

## Framework

**Demand–response–recovery timeline**

Map a fictional stressor, onset, duration, response, resources and opportunity for recovery. Mark what is known, inferred or unknown. Identify one practical action and one possible support route. Distinguish a short challenge with a clear endpoint from repeated demands without adequate recovery. Do not assign a severity score or infer a diagnosis.

## Worked example

A fictional administrator has to prepare for a one-off presentation. They feel tense before it and have time to rest afterwards. Another administrator has repeated deadlines, late changes, conflicting instructions and no reliable opportunity for recovery over several months. Both may experience stress, but the second pattern suggests continuing demands and resource problems. A constructive response includes clarifying priorities, addressing workload and seeking appropriate support if everyday functioning is affected. It is not sufficient to compare which person appears calmer.

### Contrasting case

A sudden event can be severe even if brief, while a long-running demand may be manageable when resources and recovery are adequate. Duration alone does not determine the significance or urgency of a stress response.

## Guided practice

### Classify the pattern, not the person

Scenario A: a fictional student has a difficult exam tomorrow, support and an agreed rest period afterwards. Scenario B: a worker receives conflicting instructions every week and cannot complete all tasks within agreed hours. Scenario C: someone experiences sudden serious physical symptoms and assumes it is stress. Identify the likely pattern, unknowns and appropriate next action.

### Worked solution

A: time-limited demand; plan preparation and rest while remaining attentive to support needs. B: repeated demand with resource mismatch; clarify priorities and seek organisational support. C: do not attribute serious symptoms to stress; seek urgent medical assessment as appropriate. The exercise cannot determine individual diagnoses.

Attempt the task before checking the worked solution. Identify one justified correction, then repeat using an equivalent fictional case. The criterion is quality of reasoning, not personal performance or symptom change.

## Independent transfer

Draw two fictional demand-response-recovery timelines. Identify a stressor, an observable response, a resource and a possible organisational change for each. Explain why duration is insufficient to judge severity.

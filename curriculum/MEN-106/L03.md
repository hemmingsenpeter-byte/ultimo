# MEN-106 / L03 — Coping choices

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Apply coping choices in the context of stress management.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall your demand-resource matrix. A fictional learner has an unresolved scheduling conflict and also feels unsettled while waiting for a response. Which actions address the problem and which support functioning while it remains unresolved?

## Core teaching

Coping refers to efforts to manage a challenging situation or its effects. One useful distinction is between problem-directed actions, which attempt to change a demand, and response-directed actions, which help a person manage their experience while the situation remains. These can complement one another. Neither category is inherently superior; the relevant question is what is feasible, safe and appropriate given the circumstances. A trusted person or professional may help identify options beyond those initially visible.

An unresolved task conflict might be addressed by asking for a decision, gathering missing information or renegotiating a deadline. A comfortable pause, an ordinary enjoyable activity or a supportive conversation can offer relief without directly changing the conflict. A temporary response-focused action is not necessarily avoidance. It becomes unsuitable if it repeatedly prevents necessary action or substitutes for support when functioning is significantly impaired. Do not treat coping as an obligation to eliminate all discomfort.

WHO offers general stress-management resources for coping with adversity. The worksheet here is an original educational exercise, not a reproduced therapeutic programme. Use only comfortable, voluntary activities; a learner may decline any exercise. Avoid instructions that deliberately provoke distress, demand private disclosure or involve breath restriction, substances or unsafe physical activity. In a threatening situation, prioritise safety and appropriate assistance over self-management.

## Framework

### Match response to controllability and urgency

1. Check safety and urgent obligations first.
2. Identify what can be changed now, later, or only with assistance.
3. Choose one feasible problem-directed action if appropriate.
4. Choose one safe, optional response-directed support such as a pause or contacting a trusted person.
5. Set a review point: did the demand change, is functioning manageable, or is additional support needed?

This is a practical choice model, not treatment; the two action types can occur together.

## Worked example

A fictional employee receives two competing deadlines. They ask the responsible manager to decide which takes priority and send a concise account of the resource gap. While waiting, they take an agreed ordinary break and then work on the clearly authorised task. The request addresses the problem; the break supports manageable functioning. If no decision arrives and a safety-critical obligation is threatened, they use the agreed escalation route. The case does not require the employee to become perfectly calm before acting.

### Contrasting case

A person experiencing threats or abuse should not be instructed to reframe the situation until it feels tolerable. Safety planning and specialist support may be required. A generic coping exercise is not an appropriate substitute.

## Guided practice

### Choose paired actions

A fictional student has an inaccessible study platform and an approaching deadline. Identify an action addressing the access problem, an optional support while waiting, and a review condition. Then compare a case where the obstacle is outside the student's control for several days.

### Worked solution

Request an accessible alternative or deadline adjustment through the appropriate channel; record what is needed without exposing private information. While waiting, choose a manageable available activity or speak with a trusted person. If access remains unavailable, follow up with the responsible service rather than repeatedly trying the same inaccessible task. For an uncontrollable delay, arrange alternatives and preserve recovery. Feedback: distinguish action from waiting and do not make emotional disclosure a prerequisite.

Attempt the task before checking the worked solution. Identify one justified correction, then repeat using an equivalent fictional case. The criterion is quality of reasoning, not personal performance or symptom change.

## Independent transfer

Prepare a two-column coping choice worksheet for two fictional demands, one modifiable and one currently outside the learner's control. Include safe alternatives, a review date and an escalation condition. Explain why doing more is not always the best response.

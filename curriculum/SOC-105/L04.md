# SOC-105 / L04 — Authenticity

**Draft — independent academic and subject review pending.** Outcome: Evaluate and justify decisions about authenticity in the context of charisma and humour.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall SOC-104 on confidence versus skill. A fictional learner uses a prepared introduction at an event. Does adapting their communication to the setting necessarily make them inauthentic?

## Core teaching

Authenticity is a contested concept rather than a single observable property. Psychological models have examined alignment between experienced values or beliefs and conduct, alongside concerns about external influence and self-understanding. Wood and colleagues developed one research operationalisation; this lesson does not administer that scale or assess a person's true self. A useful educational distinction is between honest, context-appropriate expression and deliberately misrepresenting one's experience, intentions or capability.

Adapting a communication style is not automatically deception. A person can prepare a concise introduction, use a professional register or choose an accessible written format while communicating truthfully. Different roles require different levels of formality and disclosure. Privacy is compatible with honesty: someone need not reveal every opinion, difficulty or personal history to be genuine. Conversely, a spontaneous statement can still be misleading or harmful.

Authenticity is not permission to disregard others. Expressing a strongly held opinion does not establish its accuracy or justify insulting someone. Check whether statements are factually supported, commitments are realistic and the purpose is transparent. Social pressure may encourage a person to exaggerate achievements or agree with a view they do not hold. A suitable response can acknowledge uncertainty, decline to comment or express disagreement respectfully. The goal is consistent, truthful and appropriate conduct rather than a performance of confidence, intimacy or personal revelation.

## Framework

**Values–facts–role–choice–follow-through**

Identify the legitimate purpose and relevant values. Verify factual claims and capacity. Choose a communication format suited to the role and audience without misleading them. Decide what information is private or unnecessary. Check whether the action is consistent with stated commitments. Revise if circumstances change; do not promise a personality transformation.

## Worked example

A fictional learner attends a professional event and is asked about experience organising community activities. They have helped arrange two events but have never managed a large programme. A truthful introduction could state the two contributions, their interest in learning about larger events and a specific question. Preparing this statement does not make it deceptive. Claiming to have led a major programme to impress the listener would be inaccurate. If asked about a personal matter unrelated to the event, the learner can decline to discuss it. Their authenticity is not assessed by how much they disclose.

### Contrasting case

A fictional participant says, 'I'm just being authentic,' after insulting another member and refusing to correct a factual error. Sincerity about an opinion does not excuse harm or make the opinion true. Appropriate accountability and respectful communication still apply.

## Guided practice

**Compare three introductions.** A: 'I have experience helping organise two community events and want to learn about larger programmes.' B: 'I'm a leading expert,' despite the same limited experience. C: 'I don't want to share private details, but I can explain my relevant work.' Evaluate factual accuracy, role fit and privacy.

**Worked solution.** A accurately describes capability and a learning goal. B overstates experience and creates a misleading impression. C sets an appropriate privacy limit while providing relevant information. A and C may be prepared in advance without compromising honesty. If the listener asks for evidence, offer an appropriate public example or clarify limits rather than inventing credentials.

Now revise a fictional promise to introduce someone to a senior decision-maker when permission has not been obtained. Say you can ask whether they would welcome an introduction, without guaranteeing it. Follow-through must match the promise.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify a specific correction and repeat with an equivalent fictional case. A useful response states why the choice fits the circumstances, what remains uncertain and what would change the choice.

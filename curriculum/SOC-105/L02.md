## Orientation and retrieval

Recall the distinction between intent and impact from EMO-104. A fictional group laughs after a joke about a newcomer, but the newcomer remains silent. What can and cannot be concluded from the laughter?

## Core teaching

Humour is a broad family of expressive practices that can produce amusement, surprise, playfulness or shared recognition. It depends on language, relationships, timing and context. Martin and colleagues' humour-styles research distinguishes uses that support relationships, help oneself manage ordinary difficulties, elevate oneself at others' expense, or seek social connection at one's own expense. These are categories of self-reported tendencies, not a complete taxonomy of jokes or a licence to score individuals. A joke can have different meanings for different listeners.

Affiliative humour can create a shared moment without assigning a target; an observation about a harmless shared situation may be easier to understand than sarcasm about a person. Aggressive humour can demean or exclude, and self-directed humour may become uncomfortable if a person feels they must belittle themselves to belong. Intent does not settle impact. Laughter may reflect amusement, politeness or pressure; silence does not establish offence or enjoyment. Power differences matter because a subordinate may feel unable to object to a supervisor's joke.

Choose humour in relation to the actual audience and purpose. It is optional. Avoid jokes that exploit private details, demean protected characteristics, threaten or humiliate. Do not treat a refusal to laugh as a social failure. In serious or safety-sensitive circumstances, plain information may be preferable. If someone identifies an inappropriate impact, acknowledge the specific issue, stop or correct the behaviour, and follow applicable procedures if necessary. A demand that they accept the joke is not repair.

## Framework

**Purpose–target–context–impact–response**

Identify why humour is being used. Ask who or what is the target, whether the context supports play, and who might feel pressured. Compare a harmless shared observation with a joke directed at a person. Choose plain speech if the benefits are unclear or risks high. If a concern is expressed, listen, stop the problematic behaviour and address the impact proportionately. Do not grade laughter.

## Worked example

A fictional team prepares for a meeting after a projector fails. A participant says, 'The slides seem to have taken an early holiday,' and then explains the backup plan. The joke targets a temporary shared inconvenience, not someone's competence. A contrasting joke blaming a new employee personally for an unverified failure could embarrass them and distract from the facts. Even the first joke may be poorly timed if the failure has serious consequences; the participant should prioritise accurate information. The criterion is relevance and low risk rather than a successful punchline.

### Contrasting case

A fictional manager repeatedly jokes about an employee's accent and claims the jokes are harmless because coworkers laugh. Their laughter does not establish voluntary approval, and power may inhibit objection. Stop the conduct and use appropriate organisational processes where needed.

## Guided practice

**Evaluate three fictional options.** A: playful comment about a harmless shared scheduling mix-up. B: a joke exposing a colleague's private financial situation. C: a self-directed joke implying the speaker is worthless. Identify target, purpose, risk and alternative.

**Worked solution.** A may be suitable if the situation is ordinary and the group welcomes play, but still state the correct schedule. B exposes private information and targets someone; omit it. C may put the speaker under pressure to belittle themselves or make others uncomfortable; an ordinary observation about the task is preferable. None requires actual performance.

Rewrite B as a harmless observation that does not depend on a person's vulnerability. If no suitable humorous version exists, give a direct statement. Explain why choosing not to joke can demonstrate sound judgement.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify a specific correction and repeat with an equivalent fictional case. A useful response states why the choice fits the circumstances, what remains uncertain and what would change the choice.

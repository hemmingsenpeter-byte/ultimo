# SOC-105 / L01 — Warmth and attention

## Core teaching

Charisma is an everyday term for perceived interpersonal appeal or influence. It is not a single settled skill or a measure of moral worth. Social perception research identifies warmth and competence as broad dimensions of how people perceive others. Warmth concerns perceived intentions, such as friendliness or trustworthiness, while competence concerns perceived capacity. These are perceptions, not proof of character or ability. A persuasive person can be mistaken, and a quiet person can be dependable. Do not infer trustworthiness from eye contact, attractiveness, vocal style or confidence.

Attention is more directly observable through actions: allowing someone to finish, referring accurately to something they said, checking an ambiguous point and remembering an agreed next step. Warmth can be communicated through respectful acknowledgment and genuine interest, but appropriate expression varies across cultures, situations and individuals. A smile, enthusiastic tone or handshake is neither required nor sufficient. Sometimes the warmest response is concise practical help or allowing someone space.

Distinguish curiosity from extracting information. Attending to someone does not mean continually asking questions, demanding disclosure or pretending to share an interest. A useful interaction has mutual choice, appropriate timing and an understandable purpose. If someone declines to continue, respect that decision rather than treating it as a challenge to overcome. Assess whether communication was accurate, respectful and relevant, not whether someone felt obliged to like the speaker.

## Framework

**Notice–acknowledge–invite–allow**

Notice what was actually communicated. Acknowledge one relevant fact or contribution. Invite a response with a proportionate question or offer. Allow refusal, a different communication format or an end to the exchange. Confirm any agreed next step. Compare perceived warmth with actual evidence of reliability rather than assuming one establishes the other.

## Worked example

At a fictional professional event, a newcomer says they work on a community garden and are looking for advice about accessible paths. One host gives a long account of their own achievements and asks for a private contact number. Another says, 'You mentioned accessible paths. Are you looking for information about design or funding?' The newcomer says design. The second host identifies a public guide and asks whether they want the reference. The person declines further conversation to attend another session. The host respects this and does not demand contact details. Attentiveness is demonstrated by accurately recognising the stated need; the outcome does not depend on creating an impression of intimacy.

### Contrasting case

A fictional speaker uses a highly engaging introduction but provides inaccurate information and pressures a listener to buy something. Perceived warmth does not establish competence or integrity. A less expressive speaker who provides accurate information and respects refusal may be more appropriate.

## Guided practice

**Compare two fictional introductions.** A: 'I know everyone here. Tell me what you do and I'll introduce you to the important people.' B: 'Welcome. I'm helping with the event schedule. Would you like information about the sessions?' Identify role clarity, assumptions, recipient choice and evidence of actual support.

**Worked solution.** A makes a status claim without evidence, asks for information without a clear purpose and implies a promise it may not be able to honour. B identifies a role, offers a defined service and allows refusal. B is not automatically trustworthy, but its proposed action is verifiable. Improve A by stating what introductions, if any, can be arranged with both parties' permission.

Now write a reply to a fictional guest who says they prefer to explore alone. A suitable response acknowledges the choice and identifies a public information point if useful; it does not keep asking questions. Assess accuracy and autonomy independently from whether the guest smiles or responds warmly.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify a specific correction and repeat with an equivalent fictional case. A useful response states why the choice fits the circumstances, what remains uncertain and what would change the choice.

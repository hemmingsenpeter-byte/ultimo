## Core teaching

A story describes events or imagined events through a connected sequence. It can make a situation concrete, help a listener follow an objective and show how a decision or obstacle mattered. A useful everyday narrative often contains a setting, a relevant goal, a change or complication, an action and a consequence. These are design choices, not compulsory stages. A short factual anecdote can be enough. The listener's purpose and available time should guide length.

Narrative transportation refers to involvement in a story's world; research investigates attention, imagination and emotion in relation to narrative engagement and persuasion. Engagement does not establish that a claim is true or representative. A vivid success case can conceal failures, alternative causes or selection effects. When a story illustrates a general claim, distinguish what actually happened, what is fictional or reconstructed, and what broader evidence supports the conclusion. Avoid embellishing results, appropriating someone else's experience or presenting hypothetical outcomes as actual achievements.

An engaging story also respects the audience and the people described. Use details relevant to the purpose; avoid identifiable private information without appropriate permission. In an ordinary conversation, allow interruption or a change of topic. In professional settings, make the point clear and disclose uncertainty. If the audience needs a decision-ready comparison, the story may be supplementary rather than the central evidence. Assess coherence, accuracy and relevance, not emotional manipulation.

## Framework

**Setting–goal–change–action–result–meaning**

Establish the relevant context and objective. Describe a concrete complication, the action taken and the known result. Separate fact from interpretation, and state one bounded takeaway. Identify a limitation or alternative explanation if making a wider claim. Remove irrelevant private details and offer a concise version. Text model: sequence explains the case; evidence supports any generalisation.

## Worked example

A fictional volunteer tells a story about improving event attendance. Their initial version: 'Everyone loved my new system; it transformed the group.' A better version states that two earlier events had twelve and fourteen registrations, that the organiser introduced a clearer invitation, and that the next event received eighteen registrations. The result is an observed difference, not proof the invitation caused it: timing and activity may have changed. The speaker explains the practical lesson, clearer logistics can reduce uncertainty, and suggests testing it again. The anecdote is concrete without turning one observation into a universal claim.

### Contrasting case

A fictional salesperson recounts one dramatic customer success and omits known failures, then claims all users will achieve the same outcome. The story may be persuasive but is misleading. A representative comparison and qualified evidence are needed.

## Guided practice

**Edit a fictional story.** Facts: a community event had an unclear venue notice; four attendees went to the wrong room; an organiser added a room number and map to the next invitation; no wrong-room arrivals were reported at that next event. Write a 60-second story and a one-sentence takeaway.

**Worked solution.** 'At one event, our notice didn't identify the room clearly, and four attendees went to the wrong place. For the next event, we added the room number and a map. We received no reports of wrong-room arrivals that time. Clearer venue information was a reasonable improvement, although we can't know whether the new notice alone caused the change.' The story states the relevant facts, action and limitation.

Compare the takeaway with 'Maps eliminate every access problem.' The latter overgeneralises and ignores other access barriers. A useful revision proposes a follow-up check with attendees and an accessible alternative to the map.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify a specific correction and repeat with an equivalent fictional case. A useful response states why the choice fits the circumstances, what remains uncertain and what would change the choice.

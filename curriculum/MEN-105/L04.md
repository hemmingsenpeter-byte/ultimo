# MEN-105 / L04 — Recovery

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Evaluate and justify decisions about recovery in the context of resilience.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the idea of adaptation over time. Imagine a fictional person returning to ordinary responsibilities after a disruptive period. Why might a plan need to change even when a first week goes well?

## Core teaching

Recovery is a process, not a deadline or a requirement to return to exactly the previous level of functioning. Everyday recovery can involve time away from demands, supportive relationships, sleep opportunity, adjustments to workload and valued activities. Clinical recovery or rehabilitation may require specialist assessment and guidance; this lesson does not prescribe a schedule following illness, trauma or injury. People may experience uneven progress and changes across domains.

Sonnentag and Fritz's occupational recovery questionnaire distinguishes psychological detachment from work, relaxation, mastery experiences and control over leisure time. These are categories of experience examined in occupational research, not mandatory activities or a universal prescription. A hobby may offer challenge and enjoyment for one person but feel like an extra demand for another. The individual's autonomy, access and circumstances matter. Sleep and rest should not be traded away simply to achieve a productivity target.

A recovery plan should distinguish essential responsibilities, optional demands, resources and signs that the plan is no longer manageable. Set a modest review point and adjust based on functional evidence and the person's wishes. Avoid interpreting setbacks as proof of failure or using a classroom checklist to certify readiness for work, exercise or safety-sensitive duties. Persistent distress or impaired functioning may warrant qualified assessment, and immediate danger requires emergency help. The student's assessed skill is planning and reasoning, not their personal health outcome.

## Framework

**Stabilise–support–reintroduce–review**

Identify immediate needs and non-negotiable safety constraints. Arrange accessible support and reduce unnecessary demands where possible. Consider one manageable ordinary activity chosen by the fictional person, with appropriate professional input where needed. Review feasibility, functioning and outstanding demands; adapt or seek additional help. No fixed timetable, performance quota or medical clearance is implied.

## Worked example

A fictional volunteer coordinator has experienced a disruptive venue closure. Essential communication and safeguarding duties remain, but an optional redesign can be delayed. They negotiate temporary workload sharing, protect ordinary rest and choose a manageable enjoyable activity in their available time. At the review point, the venue remains closed and the workload is still too high. The plan changes: the responsible committee is asked to authorise a revised schedule. This is adaptation to evidence, not failure to recover quickly. No assumption is made about the person's clinical status.

### Contrasting case

A person returning after an illness or injury may have formal return-to-work restrictions or clinician advice. A generic resilience worksheet cannot override those requirements or assess fitness. A good-looking productivity log does not establish readiness.

## Guided practice

**Compare two fictional recovery plans.** Plan A schedules all postponed duties immediately and measures success by hours worked. Plan B prioritises essential duties, arranges support, preserves rest and reviews progress after an agreed interval. Identify benefits, costs and unresolved information for both.

**Worked solution.** A may reduce a backlog but risks overload and treats hours as the outcome. B protects feasibility and creates an adjustment point, but depends on authorised workload changes and available support. A suitable plan should specify who can approve changes, which activities are optional, and when professional advice is needed. Neither plan can guarantee recovery or replace clinical guidance.

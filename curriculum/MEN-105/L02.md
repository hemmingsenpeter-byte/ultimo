# MEN-105 / L02 — Actionable stressors

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Compare and distinguish actionable stressors in the context of resilience.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the difference between a resource and an outcome. In a fictional schedule disruption, separate what the learner can directly change from what depends on another person or a future decision.

## Core teaching

An actionable stressor is a demand or circumstance for which a feasible step can change exposure, consequences or available resources. Actionability is not binary. Some factors are directly controllable, some require influence or negotiation, and others are presently outside the person's authority. These categories change with new information and support. A person cannot be expected to control outcomes that depend on institutions, other people or random events.

A useful response distinguishes problem-directed action from preparation for uncertainty. For example, a learner can send a request for an accessible format but cannot guarantee when it will arrive. They can agree a fallback and review date. WHO's mental health at work guidance emphasises that organisational conditions can require organisational interventions. If a demand is unsafe or unfair, seeking support or appropriate reporting may be more constructive than accepting it as inevitable. Conversely, repeatedly attempting to change an irreversible event can consume resources that could be used for present needs.

Actionability depends on priorities and constraints. Select steps that are lawful, safe, respectful and within authority. Avoid turning every concern into an action item: sometimes waiting, rest, ordinary emotional support or professional care is appropriate. The map below is a planning aid, not a claim that dividing stressors into categories treats psychological distress. It also does not assume that a person is responsible for circumstances beyond their control.

## Framework

**Control–influence–uncertainty matrix**

For each fictional stressor, classify direct actions, actions requiring cooperation, and outcomes currently uncertain or outside control. Identify a responsible decision-maker, prerequisite and feasible first step. Add a fallback and review date. Prioritise immediate safety and essential needs before ordinary optimisation. If action would be unsafe or beyond competence, refer instead.

## Worked example

A fictional project worker is told a site will be unavailable for an unknown period. They can inventory tasks that do not require access. They can request clarification and negotiate a revised sequence with the authorised manager. They cannot guarantee when access resumes. A fallback is to prepare alternative tasks and schedule a review when updated information is due. The worker communicates uncertainty honestly rather than promising an impossible deadline. If access restrictions relate to safety, they do not bypass them to demonstrate initiative.

### Contrasting case

A worker facing an unsafe situation should not categorise it as 'outside my control' and simply endure it when an appropriate safety reporting route exists. Equally, reporting does not guarantee the outcome or make the worker personally responsible for resolving every institutional failure.

## Guided practice

**Sort the decision space.** A fictional student has a broken laptop, a deadline in two days and an uncertain replacement date. List three direct actions, two actions requiring cooperation, and one uncertain outcome. Identify a safety/privacy boundary.

**Worked solution.** Direct: review available files, list essential deliverables, prepare a request. Cooperation: arrange an authorised loan device or request a deadline adjustment. Uncertain: replacement arrival. Fallback: agree a paper or accessible alternative if permitted. Protect accounts and private data; do not use unauthorised devices or bypass security. An effective answer prioritises the most consequential feasible step.

Attempt before consulting the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning gap and retry with an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning and safe boundaries, not the learner's own health or circumstances.

## Independent transfer

Apply the matrix to a new fictional adversity with a structural component. Compare two actions by authority, feasibility, likely benefit, possible harm and reversibility. State an uncertainty that remains after acting.

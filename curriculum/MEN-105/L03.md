## Orientation and retrieval

Recall the adversity-resource map. A fictional friend wants to help but offers advice when the person actually needs transport to an appointment. What makes support useful rather than merely well-intended?

## Core teaching

Social support includes different kinds of help: emotional support, practical or instrumental assistance, information, and companionship or belonging. A network's size is not the same as its accessibility, quality or relevance to a particular need. Someone may have many acquaintances but no available person to help with a specific task. Conversely, a small number of reliable relationships may provide meaningful support. Support can come from friends, family, peers, community organisations or professionals, depending on the need.

Cohen and Wills distinguish accounts in which social support has a general association with well-being from accounts in which it buffers the effects of stress. The usefulness of support can depend on how it matches the challenge and how it is measured. This classic research does not establish that every contact is helpful or that a particular number of friends guarantees resilience. Unsolicited advice, controlling assistance or dismissive interactions can be unwelcome. Cultural preferences, disability, privacy, consent and the individual's desired type of support should guide the conversation.

Requesting support is a practical skill, not a failure of independence. A useful request specifies the need, a concrete form of help, timing and room for refusal. Offering support similarly involves permission and capacity. A peer should avoid becoming the sole provider of complex or clinical care, and should not promise availability beyond their capacity. Where an individual faces serious risk or persistent impairment, appropriate professional or emergency routes may also be needed.

## Framework

**Need–match–ask–agree–review**

Identify the fictional need and its urgency. Match it to a support type and appropriate person/service. Make a clear, time-bounded, non-coercive request. Agree what is feasible, including privacy and boundaries. Identify an alternative if the request is declined. Review whether the support meets the need without treating acceptance as an entitlement.

## Worked example

A fictional learner has a disrupted schedule and is unsure which assignment requirements still apply. A friend offers reassurance, which may be welcome, but cannot clarify the official requirements. The learner asks the educator for an authorised deadline and rubric, and asks a friend whether they are available for a short study-planning conversation. These requests match informational and emotional needs to different people. If the friend declines, the learner can seek another appropriate resource rather than demanding a justification. The educator's role is not clinical care.

### Contrasting case

An offer to take over someone's entire schedule without permission may reduce their autonomy. A person may prefer quiet companionship, practical help or no immediate conversation. The existence of a relationship does not establish consent for every type of assistance.

## Guided practice

**Match support to need.** Scenario A: a fictional person needs transport to an appointment. B: someone is unsure how to submit a form. C: someone asks to talk after a difficult day. D: someone reports persistent impairment. Draft one bounded request for each and identify a fallback.

**Worked solution.** A: request a specific ride if feasible, or investigate accessible transport. B: contact the responsible information provider. C: ask a trusted person for a conversation and check their availability. D: offer help accessing an appropriately qualified professional, while maintaining ordinary support if wanted. Immediate danger requires emergency assistance. Feedback: specific, consent-based, appropriately routed requests are stronger than 'someone should help me'.

Attempt before consulting the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning gap and retry with an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning and safe boundaries, not the learner's own health or circumstances.

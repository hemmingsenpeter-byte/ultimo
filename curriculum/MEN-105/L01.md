# MEN-105 / L01 — Resilience and resources

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Explain resilience and resources in the context of resilience.

## Orientation and retrieval

Consider two fictional adults facing a similar setback but with different housing security, social support and work flexibility. Before reading, explain why their recovery paths might differ without assuming one has more character.

## Core teaching

Resilience is often discussed as adaptation over time in the context of adversity. Research uses several definitions: some focus on functioning trajectories, others on processes or resources associated with adaptation. It is not a single fixed personality attribute, an absence of emotional pain or a guarantee of returning to a previous state. A person can function well in one domain and struggle in another. The relevant timeframe and definition of adaptation must be made explicit.

Resources can operate at many levels: personal skills, supportive relationships, cultural and community connections, stable housing, accessible services, time, financial security and institutional arrangements. Their usefulness depends on the adversity and circumstances. Southwick and colleagues' interdisciplinary discussion emphasises the multiple levels involved in understanding resilience. This matters because a course about resilience should not place the entire burden of adaptation on the individual. Reducing preventable adversity may be more effective and fairer than asking people to tolerate it.

Distinguish a protective resource from an outcome. A reliable support network may be a resource; maintaining or recovering everyday functioning may be an outcome. A resource is not guaranteed to work for every person, and a difficult outcome does not prove a lack of resilience. This course uses fictional planning cases to examine options and support. It does not ask students to demonstrate toughness, describe traumatic experiences or achieve a clinical recovery target.

## Framework

**Adversity–resources–adaptation map**

Describe the fictional challenge and timeframe. Identify relevant personal, relational, material and institutional resources, noting what is missing. Specify a modest functional goal chosen for the scenario, not a measure of worth. Identify one action that reduces adversity and one that improves access to support. Add uncertainty and a review point.

Text model: adversity interacts with available resources over time → possible adaptation trajectories; no arrow guarantees an outcome.

## Worked example

A fictional employee's workplace closes unexpectedly. They have a supportive friend but limited savings and uncertain access to transport. A resource map identifies immediate income and transport constraints, social support, transferable skills and access to employment services. Rather than declaring that a positive mindset will solve the setback, a plan identifies a practical priority: secure basic necessities and identify appropriate assistance while exploring suitable work. Adaptation may involve creating a new routine, not restoring the exact previous role. The timeline remains uncertain.

### Contrasting case

A fictional person appears calm after adversity but lacks access to essential resources. Calm behaviour does not establish resilience or remove the need for help. Another person expresses distress while gradually rebuilding ordinary routines; distress does not disprove adaptation.

## Guided practice

**Compare resource profiles.** Two fictional students lose access to their study space. A has alternative workspace and transport; B has neither but has a supportive teacher. List the adversity, resources, missing resources and two proportionate responses for each.

**Worked solution.** A may arrange the available workspace; B may need accessible study space or a revised timetable with institutional help. Both may benefit from rest and ordinary social support. The same challenge does not imply equal opportunity. Identify a possible structural change benefiting both, such as offering flexible access. Do not grade either student on their emotional response.

Attempt before consulting the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning gap and retry with an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning and safe boundaries, not the learner's own health or circumstances.

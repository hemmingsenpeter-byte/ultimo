# INT-102 / L01 — Premises and conclusions

**Draft — independent review pending.** Outcome: Explain premises and conclusions in the context of logic and argument.

## Orientation and retrieval

When someone says a decision is obvious, what exactly are they asking you to believe, and what reasons have they offered? Take a recent fictional workplace recommendation and separate the claim from its justification.

## Core teaching

An argument is a set of statements in which some are offered as reasons for accepting another. The statements doing the supporting are premises; the supported statement is the conclusion. An inference is the connection between them. These roles are relational: a conclusion in one argument can become a premise in the next. A reason can be empirical, such as a measured repair cost, or normative, such as a policy about acceptable risk. Keep the two separate because a measurement alone cannot establish what ought to be done without a value or decision rule. Everyday speech often leaves a bridging premise unstated. Reconstructing it makes the reasoning examinable, not necessarily correct. A report that merely describes an event need not be an argument; an explanation may instead assume the event happened and try to account for why.

## Framework

Use the claim–reason–bridge map: write the conclusion in one sentence; list the explicit premises; supply the minimum plausible missing bridge, marking it as inferred; then ask whether each premise is credible and whether the support is sufficient. Text diagram: P1 + P2 → intermediate conclusion → final conclusion. If several reasons stand independently, draw separate arrows instead. Ask the speaker to confirm an inferred premise where possible.

## Worked example

A community centre manager says, “The lights are failing twice as often as last year; replacements are available; we should replace the fittings this winter.” P1 is the increased failure rate, P2 the availability of replacements, and C the recommendation. The missing bridge is that preventative replacement is preferable when reliability declines and the cost and disruption are acceptable. The facts do not establish that last condition, so the map reveals what data to collect: replacement cost, repair history and alternative options.

### Contrasting case

“The lights failed because a connector loosened” is an explanation of an accepted event, not necessarily a case for replacing every light. Treating it as a policy recommendation inserts a conclusion the speaker never offered.

## Guided practice

Map this statement: “The reading group should meet earlier because three members cannot attend at its present time.” Model: P1 three members cannot attend; C move the meeting earlier; bridge an earlier time is feasible and improves overall access without greater exclusion. Identify the missing availability information. Check that the bridge does not merely repeat the conclusion.

Attempt the task before reading the model; compare your reasoning, correct one concrete gap and retry.

## Independent transfer

Choose a public recommendation about a low-stakes issue. Write two explicit premises, a conclusion and one inferred premise; identify a fact that would weaken the argument. Revisit tomorrow without the original text.

## Knowledge check

1. What makes a statement a premise rather than a conclusion?
2. What is missing from ‘The room is cold, therefore we should buy a heater’?
3. Can a conclusion also be a premise?

1. Its role is to provide support for another statement in that argument. Name the relationship, not the location in the paragraph.
2. A bridge connecting the temperature to a purchase decision, including alternatives and acceptable cost. Distinguish the observed fact from the decision criterion.
3. Yes, an intermediate conclusion may support a further conclusion. Draw a two-stage chain.

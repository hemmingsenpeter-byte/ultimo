# INT-106 / L03 — Delays

**Draft — independent review pending.** Outcome: Apply delays in the context of systems thinking.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall the balancing queue loop. If new staff require training before productive work begins, what might happen when a manager reacts to today's backlog?

## Core teaching

A delay separates an action or state change from its observable consequence. Information delays occur when measurements arrive late; physical or implementation delays occur when actions take time to change the system. A decision rule based on stale information can overcorrect, then reverse when the delayed result appears. Even a balancing loop can oscillate when the adjustment is aggressive relative to its delay. A delay is not necessarily undesirable: training and quality checks may require time, and instant reactions to noisy data can be harmful. A useful model distinguishes leading signals such as orders placed from lagging outcomes such as stock received. Explicitly represent work in progress rather than treating promised capacity as delivered capacity.

## Framework

Create a timeline with signal date, decision date, action date and effect date. Include an in-transit stock when appropriate. Simulate two or three periods before selecting a response. Compare faster sensing, slower adjustment and shorter implementation delay; each has different costs.

## Worked example

A shop starts with 20 units and sells 5 each week. It orders 10 at the end of week one, arriving before sales in week three. Week one closes at 15; week two at 10; week three at 15 after arrival and sales. A manager who forgets the pending order might reorder again in week two and overstock when both arrive. This example isolates lead time; actual sales variability would add uncertainty.

### Contrasting case

If a delayed shipment is still below the required safety margin, simply waiting may be inappropriate. The model should compare current and projected stock against defined constraints.

## Guided practice

Simulate stock 18, consumption 4 per week, and one order of 12 arriving at the start of week four. End stocks are 14, 10, 6 and 14. Add a second order arriving at week five, then compare with a policy that tracks outstanding deliveries. Explain assumptions and identify the earliest stockout risk if delivery slips.

Attempt before reading the model; compare your reasoning and correct a specific gap. Record assumptions and ask what would change your conclusion.

# SOC-108 / L01 — Reciprocal networks

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall SOC-103 on reciprocal relationships. A fictional professional has several close colleagues and occasional contacts in other sectors. Which kinds of information might each part of the network provide, and why is a contact not merely a resource to exploit?

## Core teaching

A social network is a set of relationships connecting people or organisations. A node represents a person or entity in an analytical model; a tie represents a relationship or communication connection. Network analysis can describe patterns without treating people as interchangeable assets. Stronger ties often involve greater familiarity, interaction or mutual support, while weaker ties may connect people who interact less often. Strength is multidimensional and context dependent; it is not simply the number of messages exchanged.

Granovetter's weak-ties theory examines how connections between otherwise separated social circles can transmit information. A close group may share many information sources, while an occasional contact can connect different contexts. Rajkumar and colleagues' 2022 research used large-scale LinkedIn recommendation experiments to examine job mobility; moderately weaker connections were associated with increased transmission of job opportunities in that experimental context, with limits to the benefit of further weakness. This is not evidence that contacting strangers indiscriminately will secure a job, or that strong relationships are less valuable generally.

Ethical networking aims to build and maintain mutually chosen relationships around genuine purposes. Information, learning, practical collaboration and referrals may flow in different directions over time. Reciprocity does not require every favour to be repaid, and a connection does not establish an obligation to disclose information or provide access. Respect role boundaries, capacity, privacy and consent. Map opportunities by relevance and actual relationship rather than status or contact count. A network plan should be sustainable and compatible with the individual's preferences, not a compulsory social quota.

## Framework

**Purpose–relationship–bridge–offer–limit**

Define a legitimate learning or collaboration goal. Identify existing relationship contexts and public or voluntary opportunities to connect. Distinguish trusted close support from potential information bridges. Identify a truthful, feasible contribution or request. Check consent, privacy and capacity. Review whether the relationship remains mutually wanted rather than scoring people by usefulness.

## Worked example

A fictional property professional wants to understand accessible building operations. Their close colleagues provide practical context from the current workplace; a former classmate in another sector may know different approaches; an open professional event offers voluntary learning opportunities. The professional can ask the classmate whether a short conversation would be welcome and share a relevant public resource if useful. They do not ask for confidential client information, demand introductions or assume an occasional connection owes assistance. The event remains valuable as learning even if no new private contacts result.

### Contrasting case

A fictional learner ranks everyone by salary or influence and pursues only those who might provide a job. This ignores relationship choice and creates incentives to misrepresent interest. Ethical professional contact is transparent about purpose and does not reduce people to their status.

## Guided practice

**Analyse a synthetic network.** A learner has three close peers in the same team, two occasional contacts in different organisations and one open professional group. The goal is to learn about a new field. Map likely information overlap, possible bridges and two feasible next steps.

**Worked solution.** The close peers may offer trusted context but share some sources. Occasional contacts may bridge different organisations, although the actual information available is unknown. The open group offers a route that does not depend on private introductions. Appropriate steps include reading public materials and asking one relevant contact whether a short discussion is welcome. Do not infer that weaker ties are automatically more knowledgeable or willing.

Now compare the goal of learning new information with seeking ongoing emotional support. Different relationships may be appropriate; the weak-ties evidence about job mobility does not establish superiority for every need.

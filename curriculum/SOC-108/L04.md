# SOC-108 / L04 — Follow-up

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the low-pressure request and consent-based introduction frameworks. A fictional professional has a useful conversation and agrees to send a public reference. What makes follow-up reliable rather than intrusive?

## Core teaching

Follow-up is communication after an initial exchange intended to fulfil a commitment, clarify an outstanding decision or maintain a mutually wanted connection. It is distinct from repeated outreach aimed at overcoming refusal. A good follow-up refers to the actual exchange, identifies the relevant next step and avoids assuming a new commitment. Sending an agreed public reference, confirming a meeting time or acknowledging an introduction may be appropriate. Sending repeated unsolicited messages after a decline is not.

A networking relationship does not need a fixed contact frequency. The appropriate timing depends on the original agreement, purpose, role, urgency and recipient preferences. An unanswered optional message may reflect many circumstances; it does not establish rejection or permission to keep contacting. A single relevant reminder may be reasonable if there is no refusal and the context supports it. Then stop or use an appropriate independent route. Existing contractual or safety responsibilities may require a different authorised process; do not confuse those obligations with optional networking.

Maintain only proportionate information needed to honour commitments, such as the agreed action and date. Do not collect private details, scrape contacts or use covert behavioural profiling to maximise responses. Keep records secure and follow applicable privacy, organisational and communication requirements. Evaluate a network plan by fulfilled commitments, relevance, accuracy, choice and sustainable effort rather than the number of contacts accumulated. If circumstances change, update the person where an actual commitment exists. The learner's success does not depend on receiving a reply or creating a sale.

## Framework

**Agreement–action–timing–choice–close**

Identify what was actually agreed. Fulfil the specific commitment at the appropriate time and clearly distinguish any new request. Use a suitable communication channel and minimum necessary information. Respect refusal and stop optional outreach when it no longer serves a legitimate purpose. Record only the necessary action and review whether the arrangement is sustainable.

Text model: agreed action → fulfil or communicate change → optional next step → close unless mutually continued.

## Worked example

A fictional professional meets someone at an open event and agrees to send a public accessibility guide the following day. They send the guide with a brief note: 'Thanks for discussing venue access yesterday. Here is the public guide we mentioned. I hope it's useful.' No further response is necessary. If they also want to propose another discussion, they make it a separate optional request rather than implying that the guide created an obligation. If the guide cannot be located, they explain the delay and avoid inventing a link. The recipient's silence does not make the fulfilled commitment unsuccessful.

### Contrasting case

A fictional learner sends an optional request, receives a clear refusal and then schedules automated messages every few days. This disregards choice and changes a low-pressure contact into unwanted pursuit. Stop the optional outreach and use legitimate alternatives.

## Guided practice

**Review a fictional follow-up log.** A: promised a public link by Friday and it is now Friday. B: sent an optional request one week ago and received no answer. C: received a refusal. D: agreed to provide information that is no longer available. Decide what to do and why.

**Worked solution.** A: send the verified link or promptly communicate a real obstacle. B: depending on context, consider one brief relevant reminder or close the request; no response is not consent. C: stop optional outreach. D: explain the change honestly and offer an appropriate verified alternative if available. Preserve actual commitments without inventing new ones.

Now design a minimal fictional record: contact label, agreed action, due date, permission scope and status. Do not store personal identifiers in the exercise. Compare a fulfilled commitment with a reply-rate target: the former is within the learner's role, while the latter depends on others' choices.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify a specific correction and repeat with an equivalent fictional case. Explain why the action fits the actual context and what would change the decision.

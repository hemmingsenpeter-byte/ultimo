# SOC-108 / L03 — Consent-based introductions

**Draft — independent academic and subject review pending.** Outcome: Apply consent-based introductions in the context of networking.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall the difference between a request and an agreement. A fictional colleague offers to introduce two people whose work might overlap. What permissions are needed before sharing their contact details or creating a group message?

## Core teaching

An introduction connects two people for a stated purpose. A connector may believe that a conversation would be useful, but that belief does not establish consent from either participant. A double opt-in introduction is a practical etiquette approach: explain the proposed connection separately to each person and make the introduction only when both welcome it. This is not a universal legal requirement for every introduction; it is a useful low-pressure method when sharing contact information or creating a new private connection.

Separate permission to be introduced, permission to share particular information and agreement to take a meeting. A person may consent to sharing a public professional profile but not a private phone number. They may welcome an introductory email without committing to further conversation. Use only information appropriate to the purpose, avoid surprising someone with a group message and do not disclose confidential details. When acting for an organisation, check its actual privacy and communication obligations. New Zealand's Privacy Commissioner explains limitations on disclosure of personal information under Principle 11; lawful grounds and exceptions depend on facts. This course does not determine legal authority for a particular disclosure.

A connector should also avoid implying endorsement beyond what is known. State the relevant shared topic accurately and identify uncertainties. Neither party owes a referral, sale, interview or response. If a proposed introduction is declined, do not pressure the person or relay private reasons without permission. A respectful connector can offer a public resource instead. Evaluate whether the introduction is accurate, authorised, proportionate and voluntary, not whether the participants form a relationship.

## Framework

**Purpose–permission A–permission B–minimum information–handover**

Identify why the connection may be relevant. Ask each person separately whether they welcome an introduction and what contact details may be shared. If either declines or permission is unclear, do not make the private connection. Once both agree, share only the authorised details and briefly explain the purpose. Leave further contact to their choice. Check applicable organisational and legal duties where relevant.

## Worked example

A fictional engineer asks a former colleague whether they know anyone working on accessible event technology. The colleague knows a suitable professional and first asks whether that person would welcome a short introductory email on the topic. They confirm that a work email can be shared, not a private mobile number. The engineer also agrees to the proposed introduction. The connector writes a concise message explaining the shared interest and lets both decide whether to continue. They do not guarantee a meeting or describe either as endorsing a product. If the professional declines, the colleague can point the engineer toward a public resource without disclosing the person's reason.

### Contrasting case

A fictional connector adds an acquaintance's private mobile number to a group chat with a salesperson without asking. The perceived relevance of the introduction does not establish permission to disclose contact details or expose someone to unwanted outreach.

## Guided practice

**Assess four fictional permission records.** A: both people agree to a work-email introduction. B: one agrees and the other has not answered. C: both agree to an introduction but neither authorises sharing private phone numbers. D: a connector has a public directory link but no agreement to a private introduction.

**Worked solution.** A: introduce them using authorised work details and the stated purpose. B: wait or use a legitimate public alternative; silence is not agreement. C: use the authorised format, not private numbers. D: sharing an appropriate public directory link may be a separate option; do not present it as an endorsed personal connection. Actual privacy obligations still apply.

Now draft the introduction for A in two sentences: explain each person's relevant role and shared topic, then indicate that further conversation is optional. Avoid promises on their behalf.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify a specific correction and repeat with an equivalent fictional case. Explain why the action fits the actual context and what would change the decision.

# INT-109 / L03 — Theories of knowledge

**Draft — independent review pending.** Outcome: Apply theories of knowledge in the context of epistemology.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall why a lucky true belief may not count as knowledge. Compare a person who can articulate their evidence with someone whose reliable observation process operates without explicit reasoning.

## Core teaching

Several families of epistemological theories offer competing accounts of knowledge or justification. Evidentialist approaches connect justification to the evidence possessed by the subject. Reliabilist approaches emphasise whether belief-forming processes tend to produce true beliefs. Safety-based accounts investigate whether a belief could easily have been false in relevant nearby circumstances. Virtue epistemology highlights competent epistemic agency in different ways. These families are not mutually exclusive, and variants address different questions. An internalist–externalist distinction concerns whether relevant justificatory factors must be accessible to the subject or may depend on external features; it has several formulations. Counterexamples test proposed necessary and sufficient conditions. No single classroom example resolves the debate. Distinguish describing a position from endorsing it.

## Framework

Use a theory comparison matrix: define the condition proposed by each view; test the same case under each; locate the divergent judgement; construct a case that pressures each view; state which problem remains. Avoid presenting a family of theories as one universally agreed definition.

## Worked example

A fictional technician accurately reads an unmarked gauge using a skill developed through repeated supervised practice but cannot explain every perceptual cue. An account prioritising reliable performance may treat this favourably; an account requiring accessible evidence may ask what grounds the technician can give. A safety account asks whether similar nearby circumstances would easily yield an erroneous reading. The case invites comparison, not a definitive classification of every theory.

### Contrasting case

A learner accurately recalls a password by guessing randomly. Truth alone does not meet the ordinary motivation behind any of these additional epistemic conditions.

## Guided practice

Consider a normally accurate sensor that happens to display the correct value while malfunctioning. Compare evidence-based justification (what the observer knows about the sensor), reliability (actual process), and safety (nearby cases might be wrong). Model: the case separates apparent grounds from a reliable connection to truth. Discuss rather than forcing all accounts to the same verdict.

Attempt before reading the model; compare your reasoning and correct a specific gap. Record assumptions and ask what would change your conclusion.

## Independent transfer

Construct a new fictional case and compare three theories using the same facts. Identify a counterexample or objection and explicitly preserve disagreements.

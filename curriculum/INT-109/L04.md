# INT-109 / L04 — Confidence

**Draft — independent review pending.** Outcome: Evaluate and justify decisions about confidence in the context of epistemology.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the difference between truth and confidence. Can two people agree on a proposition's evidence yet express different confidence because of different prior assumptions?

## Core teaching

Confidence expresses the strength of belief, not whether a proposition is true. Bayesian epistemology investigates norms for degrees of belief and how evidence changes them. In a simple binary model, posterior odds equal prior odds multiplied by the likelihood ratio: how much more probable the evidence is under the hypothesis than under its alternative. A likelihood ratio above one supports the hypothesis relative to the alternative, but does not guarantee it. Priors, model choice and independence assumptions affect the result. Repeated reports from a common source are not independent likelihood updates. Numerical confidence can help expose assumptions when defensible; verbal uncertainty may be more appropriate when inputs are poorly known. Decision thresholds depend on stakes and are distinct from evidential confidence.

## Framework

Use an evidence-update ledger: proposition, prior confidence or range, new evidence, competing explanation, evidential direction and revised confidence. If using numbers, show assumptions and calculation; otherwise state which way and why confidence changes. Record what evidence would reverse the judgement.

## Worked example

A fictional equipment fault has prior probability 20% (odds 1:4). A particular indicator is twice as likely if the fault exists as if it does not, giving likelihood ratio 2. Posterior odds become 2:4 = 1:2, or probability 1/3. This is an illustrative calculation, not a real diagnostic rule. If the assumed likelihood ratio is unreliable, report a range or avoid numeric precision.

### Contrasting case

Reading three articles copied from the same notice is not equivalent to observing three independently generated tests. Multiplying the same evidence three times can exaggerate confidence.

## Guided practice

Use a prior of 50%, likelihood ratio 3, and one independent test result. Prior odds 1:1; posterior odds 3:1; posterior probability 75%. Now suppose the source is a duplicate of already considered evidence. Do not multiply again. Explain how a different prior affects the result.

# INT-109 / L01 — Belief truth and justification

**Draft — independent review pending.** Outcome: Explain belief truth and justification in the context of epistemology.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall the distinction between a conclusion and its supporting premises. Can you be justified in believing something that turns out to be false?

## Core teaching

Epistemology studies knowledge, justified belief and related norms of inquiry. A belief is an attitude of taking a proposition to be true; truth concerns whether the proposition is actually the case; justification concerns the grounds for holding the belief. These dimensions can diverge. A person may reasonably believe an erroneous report, or correctly guess an answer without sufficient grounds. A traditional analytical starting point treats propositional knowledge as justified true belief. Gettier-style cases challenge the sufficiency of that combination by showing how luck may intervene between justification and truth. Philosophers disagree about the best additional conditions and whether knowledge should be analysed in that manner at all. The lesson concerns propositions, not every form of practical or experiential knowledge.

## Framework

Create a three-column epistemic map: proposition, truth status (known from independent evidence or unresolved), and justification available to the agent at the time. Add a fourth note about the connection between evidence and truth. Distinguish a reasonable belief from an established knowledge claim.

## Worked example

A fictional caretaker receives an accurate-looking message saying the hall is closed. The message was sent in error because the hall was initially open, but it closes minutes later for a different reason before the caretaker forms the belief. The belief may be true and reasonably grounded in the apparent report, yet its connection to the actual closure is accidental. Whether it counts as knowledge is precisely the philosophical issue.

### Contrasting case

A caretaker checks the live access register, confirms the hall's closure with the responsible staff member, and records the time. This provides a stronger evidential connection, though records and testimony can still be mistaken.

## Guided practice

Classify three fictional claims: a lucky guess of a correct code; a reasonable belief based on a mistaken schedule; an accurately documented observation. Model: the first is true without adequate justification, the second justified but false, and the third has stronger grounds for knowledge subject to context.

Attempt before reading the model; compare your reasoning and correct a specific gap. Record assumptions and ask what would change your conclusion.

## Independent transfer

Build an epistemic audit of three fictional propositions, separating belief, evidence, actual status and possible epistemic luck. Include a claim whose truth remains unresolved.

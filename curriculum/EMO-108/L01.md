# EMO-108 / L01 — Perspective-taking

## Core teaching

Empathy refers to a family of processes concerned with understanding or responding to another person's experience. Research distinguishes components including perspective-taking, affective sharing and a distinction between oneself and another. Perspective-taking is an attempt to understand another person's viewpoint, information, goals and constraints. It is not mind-reading. A person can respond appropriately without feeling exactly what another feels. Compassion, understood as concern for another's welfare and willingness to help appropriately, is related but distinct.

Imagining what you would feel in someone else's situation may generate a possible interpretation, but it can also project your own expectations. The other person's preferences and circumstances may differ. A better inference uses what they actually communicate and checks meaning where appropriate. Even knowing the situation does not uniquely determine an emotional response. Culture, language, disability, power and role may influence communication, but do not infer an individual's inner experience from demographic characteristics or visible expression.

Perspective-taking has limits. A person may decline clarification, and different accounts can conflict. Understanding a viewpoint does not establish factual accuracy, justify harmful conduct or require agreement. Empathy should not be used to manipulate, pressure disclosure or evade accountability. A respectful response can leave an interpretation unresolved while addressing legitimate practical needs. The assessed skill is accurate, tentative reasoning from fictional information, not a score of personal emotional sensitivity.

## Framework

**Facts–perspective–hypothesis–check**

Record what was actually said or observed. Map the person's stated goal, constraints and available information. Generate a tentative interpretation and at least one alternative. Ask a respectful clarification if appropriate and wanted; revise or leave uncertainty unresolved. Choose a response suited to the confirmed information.

Text model: communicated facts → tentative perspective → optional clarification → revised understanding. Do not present the tentative step as fact.

## Worked example

A fictional colleague opposes a proposed schedule. One learner assumes the colleague dislikes innovation. Another notes that the colleague said the new time conflicts with an agreed responsibility. The second learner asks whether the timing is the primary issue or whether another condition should be considered. The colleague explains that an accessible alternative time would solve the conflict. The team can consider alternatives without requiring a personal explanation or assuming the colleague will endorse the final decision. If the colleague declines to explain further, the team can still consider the stated scheduling constraint. Empathy here concerns accurate attention to the communicated perspective.

### Contrasting case

A person smiles during a difficult discussion. The smile alone does not establish enjoyment, indifference, deception or confidence. It can have many meanings. Avoid interpreting appearance as definitive evidence of an internal state.

## Guided practice

**Compare two interpretations.** A fictional student misses a group discussion and reports unreliable transport. Interpretation A: 'They do not care about the group.' Interpretation B: 'Transport may have prevented attendance; an accessible alternative may help.' Identify the facts, uncertainties and a respectful question.

**Worked solution.** B is compatible with the reported barrier but remains provisional. A attributes motivation without evidence. A question might ask whether a different meeting arrangement would help. The actual impact of the missed commitment can be discussed separately, without demanding private transport information. If later evidence reveals another reason, update the interpretation.

Now consider the perspective of the remaining group members, who report having extra work. Their concern is also relevant. An empathetic response can recognise the access barrier and the workload impact together, then seek an agreed allocation rather than declaring one side's experience invalid.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning or communication gap and repeat with a novel fictional case. Assess task quality, not personal emotional experiences.

## Independent transfer

Create a fictional perspective map for two people with competing objectives. Describe each person's stated information and constraints, two unknowns and a respectful clarification. Identify one practical response that remains appropriate under uncertainty.

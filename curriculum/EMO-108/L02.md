# EMO-108 / L02 — Reflective listening

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Compare and distinguish reflective listening in the context of empathy.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall why a listener should not assume another person's experience. A fictional speaker says, 'The plan keeps changing and I cannot organise my work.' How could a listener demonstrate understanding without immediately offering advice?

## Core teaching

Reflective listening involves responding to communicated content with a concise restatement or tentative interpretation, leaving room for correction. A content reflection focuses on the substance; a meaning or feeling reflection tentatively addresses what matters to the speaker or how they describe an experience. A summary gathers several points into a coherent account. These differ from repeating words mechanically, asking an excessive sequence of questions, giving unsolicited advice or claiming certainty about hidden motives.

SAMHSA's clinical training guidance on motivational interviewing discusses reflective listening as a way to test interpretations through the speaker's response. This is a clinical context, not a qualification to deliver therapy. The ordinary communication exercise here uses the narrower skill of checking meaning and revising understanding. A written exchange, silence or accessible format may be more suitable for some people than a particular face-to-face style. Eye contact and vocal fluency are not required indicators of listening skill.

A listener should not use reflection as covert persuasion or a way to extract private information. If the speaker corrects an interpretation, update it rather than defend the original guess. Sometimes a person wants a practical answer, so reflecting indefinitely without responding is unhelpful. Ask whether an offer of assistance is wanted. Maintain actual safety, privacy and role obligations. Clinical assessment and treatment require qualified training. The objective is an accurate, useful and voluntary exchange, not a therapeutic result.

## Framework

**Hear–reflect–check–respond**

Listen to the communicated substance. Offer a short content reflection or tentative interpretation. Invite correction without requiring disclosure. Update the understanding and ask what type of response is wanted if unclear. Confirm any agreed action.

Example structure: 'It sounds as though the repeated changes make planning difficult. Have I understood that correctly?' A correction is useful information, not a failure.

## Worked example

A fictional learner says: 'I prepared for the original deadline, but the requirements changed, and now I don't know what counts.' A content reflection might be: 'The requirements changed after you prepared.' A tentative meaning reflection might be: 'Is the uncertainty about the standard the main issue?' The speaker corrects this: 'Actually, the timing is the problem; I understand the new standard.' The listener updates: 'So the revised deadline is what makes this difficult.' They then ask whether help identifying the authorised schedule would be useful. The revised reflection is better grounded than the first and does not require the listener to infer a hidden feeling.

### Contrasting case

A listener says, 'You're obviously angry because you dislike the coordinator.' This assigns an emotion and motive without evidence. A better reflection remains close to the actual statement and allows correction.

## Guided practice

**Practise with a fictional dialogue.** Speaker: 'I asked for help twice, nobody was available, and I finished the task late.' Write a content reflection, a tentative meaning reflection and one follow-up question. Then suppose the speaker clarifies that they were relieved rather than upset.

**Worked solution.** Content: 'You couldn't get help when you asked and finished later than planned.' Meaning: 'Finding available support seems to have been difficult.' Follow-up: 'Would identifying a reliable contact for next time help?' When the speaker reports relief, acknowledge the correction instead of continuing to suggest frustration. The criterion is accuracy, relevance and responsiveness to correction.

Try a second version in writing: summarise the two reported events, ask one question and avoid adding unreported emotions. Compare whether the meaning is preserved across formats. Do not grade the speaker's willingness to elaborate.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning or communication gap and repeat with a novel fictional case. Assess task quality, not personal emotional experiences.

# EMO-108 / L03 — Empathy versus agreement

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Apply empathy versus agreement in the context of empathy.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the distinction between perspective-taking and factual verification. A fictional team member believes a rule was applied unfairly. Can a listener acknowledge their concern while disagreeing about the facts?

## Core teaching

Empathy concerns understanding or responding to another person's experience. Agreement concerns endorsing a proposition, evaluation or proposed action. Acknowledging an experience does not establish that every associated interpretation is correct. A person may genuinely find a decision frustrating while the evidence about its cause remains incomplete. Conversely, disagreement about a factual claim does not require dismissing an experience. This distinction allows people to communicate accurately despite conflicting perspectives.

Separate four elements: the reported experience, the factual claim, the underlying value or practical need, and the requested action. Each may justify a different response. A listener might acknowledge frustration, independently check the timetable, agree that predictability matters and decline an unreasonable request. State uncertainty honestly rather than pretending agreement. This is not a method of covert persuasion: the other person may continue to disagree and does not owe acceptance.

Understanding the possible reason for harmful conduct does not excuse it or require the affected person to provide support, forgive or reconcile. If someone reports discrimination, threats or safety concerns, those issues need appropriate consideration and routes, not dismissal to maintain harmony. Empathy must not become forced neutrality between legitimate safety needs and coercive conduct. A person may decline engagement when it is unsafe. The course evaluates honest, respectful responses to fictional disagreements, not the learner's capacity to feel or their success in persuading another person.

## Framework

**Acknowledge–separate–evaluate–respond**

Recognise what the person actually communicates. Separate emotion, factual claim, need and proposed action. Identify evidence and actual constraints; say where you agree, disagree or remain uncertain. Select an appropriate action or verification route. Preserve autonomy, accountability and safety.

Text model: acknowledge experience ≠ endorse every explanation or request.

## Worked example

A fictional committee member says, 'Nobody considered accessibility when choosing the new venue.' The listener acknowledges the concern and checks the decision record. The record shows that some accessibility checks occurred, but one essential requirement was omitted. A response might be: 'I understand the concern about the missing requirement. The record shows some checks, but this one wasn't addressed. We can raise that specific gap with the coordinator.' The listener corrects the overstatement without dismissing the legitimate concern. The next step targets evidence and impact, not the speaker's supposed emotional state.

### Contrasting case

A fictional friend demands private messages as proof of loyalty. Another person can acknowledge their reported insecurity without agreeing to the demand. Empathy does not remove privacy or consent boundaries, and direct discussion may not be safe in a coercive situation.

## Guided practice

**Analyse a fictional complaint:** 'The feedback was unfair; the tutor hates me; delete the assessment.' Identify the reported experience, factual assertions, evidence required and requested action.

**Worked solution.** The learner reports experiencing the feedback as unfair. Whether it was unfair and whether the tutor dislikes them are separate factual or interpretive claims. Deleting an assessment may be outside the listener's authority. A suitable response acknowledges the concern, offers to compare feedback against the published rubric, and identifies an authorised appeal route if relevant. Do not endorse the motive claim or guarantee a decision. If actual misconduct is evidenced, use the appropriate process.

Now compare a response that immediately agrees with every assertion to maintain rapport against one that dismisses the whole complaint because the motive is unsupported. Both fail to separate the elements. A better response preserves the legitimate concern and evaluates evidence independently.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Correct one specific reasoning or communication gap and repeat with a novel fictional case. Assess task quality, not personal emotional experiences.

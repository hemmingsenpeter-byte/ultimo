## Core teaching

A causal claim concerns a contrast: what outcome would differ if the exposure changed while the relevant comparison conditions were held appropriately constant. We cannot observe the same unit at the same moment under both alternatives, so study designs attempt to approximate that missing counterfactual. Association can arise from causation, a common cause, reverse direction, selection or coincidence. A randomised experiment can support a causal estimate when allocation, adherence, measurement and analysis are handled appropriately. Observational studies may also inform causation, but depend on defensible assumptions and design; statistical adjustment cannot automatically remove unmeasured confounding. A mechanism makes a causal account more plausible but is not sufficient evidence by itself. Distinguish whether an intervention worked in a particular study from whether it will work elsewhere.

## Framework

Use contrast–diagram–design–qualification. State the intervention and comparison; sketch plausible causal arrows and common causes; choose a design and list its assumptions; qualify the conclusion by population, time, measurement and uncertainty. Ask what would make the apparent effect disappear. This qualitative tool does not replace a formal causal identification analysis.

## Worked example

A shop introduces new signs in December and reports increased sales relative to November. Holiday demand may drive both the decision to install signs and the sales rise. Compare similar sites randomly allocated signs at the same time in a hypothetical study; define the outcome and check whether pricing or stock changed. A positive difference would support an effect within the tested conditions, not prove that signs explain all December growth.

### Contrasting case

A before-and-after difference is not worthless, but without a credible comparison it may be unable to distinguish the intervention from seasonality or another concurrent change.

## Guided practice

Evaluate ‘More library events cause higher membership because libraries with more events have more members.’ Model: large libraries may have more funding, enabling both. Existing membership may also drive demand for events. Propose a staggered, randomly allocated pilot if feasible; otherwise document differences, historical trends and assumptions. Phrase the observational result as an association until stronger evidence is available.

Attempt the task before reading the model; compare your reasoning, correct one concrete gap and retry.

## Independent transfer

Take a fictional causal headline, construct two rival diagrams and propose a design that discriminates between them. Specify which conclusion would still be unwarranted even after a positive test.

## Knowledge check

1. What is the counterfactual in a causal comparison?
2. Why can an observational association be misleading?
3. Does a causal result automatically generalise?

<details><summary>Answers (open after attempting)</summary>

1. The outcome under the alternative exposure or intervention for the relevant unit or population. Name the comparison precisely.
2. Confounding, reverse causation, selection or chance can generate it. Supply one plausible alternative path.
3. No; other populations, settings or implementations may respond differently. State the study boundary.

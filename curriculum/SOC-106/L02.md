## Orientation and retrieval

Recall INT-105 on bias. A fictional meeting has seven participants who support a proposal and one who raises a factual concern. Is agreement among the seven independent evidence that their proposal is correct?

## Core teaching

Conformity concerns adjusting behaviour or judgement in response to group influence. Normative influence refers to pressure or desire to fit in or avoid disapproval. Informational influence concerns treating others' judgements as evidence about what is correct. Both can operate together. Classic Asch experiments examined judgement under majority pressure, but the observed rates depend on experimental design, participants and context. Do not treat one famous result as a universal prediction of how people behave.

Coordination through agreement is often useful: a group can follow an agreed meeting procedure without independently re-evaluating every convention. Information from knowledgeable people can also improve judgement. The problem arises when apparent consensus substitutes for evidence, suppresses relevant concerns or leads people to violate duties. A unanimous decision may reflect genuine agreement, incomplete information, pressure, dependence on one source or lack of opportunity to dissent. Silence does not prove consent.

A group can separate independent assessment from collective discussion, explicitly invite counterevidence and record unresolved concerns. Psychological safety, as studied by Edmondson, concerns perceived safety for interpersonal risk taking and is associated with learning behaviour in studied teams. It does not mean removing accountability or accepting all claims. A useful process allows correction and respectful disagreement while preserving decision authority. Where a concern involves actual safety or serious misconduct, use authorised procedures rather than relying on informal majority approval.

## Framework

**Independent view–evidence–dissent–decision**

Before discussion, identify the task criteria and invite independent assessments where useful. Compare the reasons, not just the number of supporters. Ask for contrary evidence and clarify uncertainty. Identify who decides and record dissent or unresolved conditions. Follow safety and legal obligations regardless of consensus. Review after new evidence becomes available.

## Worked example

A fictional group chooses a venue. Six participants favour option A because it is popular; one notes that its capacity may be below the required number. The facilitator asks for the published capacity and checks it against the expected attendance. If the concern is confirmed, A may be infeasible despite popularity. If the figure was outdated, the group can revise the objection. The process treats dissent as information, not disloyalty. The authorised decision-maker records the criterion and reason for the final choice.

### Contrasting case

A group agrees to begin meetings on time. Following this norm can be useful coordination and does not necessarily involve abandoning independent judgement. Conversely, resisting every group convention is not evidence of critical thinking.

## Guided practice

**Evaluate a fictional decision.** Four members support supplier A after seeing the same advertisement. Two support B based on comparable cost data. A seventh has access information that has not been discussed. Identify possible influence mechanisms and propose a fair process.

**Worked solution.** The four opinions may share a common source rather than independent evidence. The two cost comparisons need checking, and access may be a hard constraint. Request independent criteria-based assessments, hear the access evidence and compare costs on the same basis. If the group has an authorised voting process, use it after the constraints are understood. Do not assume the minority is necessarily correct.

Now distinguish public agreement from private judgement: a member may publicly agree because discussion has ended or because of social pressure. The record should not invent their private view. Provide an opportunity to register an objection if appropriate.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify a specific correction and repeat with an equivalent fictional case. A useful response states why the choice fits the circumstances, what remains uncertain and what would change the choice.

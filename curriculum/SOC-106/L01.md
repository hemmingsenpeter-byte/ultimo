# SOC-106 / L01 — Roles and norms

**Draft — independent academic and subject review pending.** Outcome: Explain roles and norms in the context of group dynamics.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve SOC-102's descriptive and injunctive norms. A fictional committee has a chair, a recorder and an influential member with no formal position. Who has authority to decide, and who merely influences discussion?

## Core teaching

A group is a collection of interacting people with some shared task, interest or relationship. Group structure includes roles, norms, status and decision arrangements. A role describes expected actions or responsibilities attached to a position; it may be formally assigned or emerge informally. A role is not a personality type. The same person can coordinate one task and contribute as a member on another. Status describes relative social position or influence, which does not automatically confer formal authority.

Norms are shared or perceived expectations about conduct. As SOC-102 explains, actual behaviour and approval can differ. Explicit requirements may be documented while informal expectations arise from repeated interaction. Group roles and norms can help coordinate work, but unclear arrangements can also produce duplication, unmet duties or exclusion. A group may assume someone will take notes because they have done it before, even when they have not agreed to do so.

A useful group map distinguishes purpose, responsibility, authority and information flow. Decision authority should be clear before participation is invited; a consultation is not necessarily a vote. Conflicts may reflect unclear responsibilities or incompatible resources rather than personality. Avoid assigning roles based on stereotypes or treating informal influence as permission to disregard rules. Where safety, privacy or regulated duties apply, authorised procedures take priority. The educational aim is to make coordination explicit, not to manipulate status.

## Framework

**Purpose–role–authority–dependency**

Define the group's intended output. List roles and their specific deliverables. Identify who can decide, who must be consulted and who needs information. Separate observed practices from actual requirements. Check capacity and consent for additional duties. Resolve gaps or conflicting ownership through an authorised route. Text model: purpose → roles → authority → coordinated deliverables.

## Worked example

A fictional community committee plans a small event. The chair facilitates discussion, the treasurer can authorise expenditure within the agreed budget, and a volunteer gathers venue options. An experienced member often proposes decisions but has no delegated spending authority. The group clarifies that the volunteer compares venues, the treasurer confirms funds and the committee follows its agreed decision process. This prevents the influential member's preference from becoming an unauthorised commitment. A note records decision ownership and next actions. The group may rotate the recording role if participants agree and have access.

### Contrasting case

A fictional team assumes that its quietest member should handle administration because they rarely object. Silence does not establish consent or capacity. Clarify and allocate actual responsibilities fairly instead of assigning duties through personality assumptions.

## Guided practice

**Map a fictional event team.** A: coordinator with booking authority; B: member comparing venues; C: member collecting access needs; D: adviser with relevant experience. The group must choose a venue within budget. Specify deliverables, decision owner, consultation needs and a norm that could create confusion.

**Worked solution.** B provides comparable venue options; C provides relevant access requirements without unnecessary private details; D offers advice. A confirms the decision under the actual delegated authority and informs the group. An informal norm that D always speaks last may be useful or may suppress others; verify its function. The final decision should not rely solely on status.

Now add a constraint: only the treasurer can approve spending above a threshold. Revise the map to show the additional dependency. Do not assume the coordinator can override it.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify a specific correction and repeat with an equivalent fictional case. A useful response states why the choice fits the circumstances, what remains uncertain and what would change the choice.

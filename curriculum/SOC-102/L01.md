# SOC-102 / L01 — Ambiguous cues

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Explain ambiguous cues in the context of social awareness.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall EMO-108's distinction between perspective-taking and mind-reading. A fictional colleague gives a short reply, 'Fine.' List three different meanings before deciding what to do. The goal is to interpret social cues proportionately rather than read hidden intentions.

## Core teaching

A social cue is an observable feature of communication or behaviour that may convey information: words, timing, tone, gesture, turn-taking or the surrounding situation. A cue is ambiguous when several explanations fit it. A short reply may indicate agreement, haste, uncertainty, irritation or an ordinary writing style. The cue itself is not evidence enough to choose confidently among these explanations. In particular, isolated gestures or gaze patterns do not provide reliable access to someone's private thoughts.

Research on email communication by Kruger and colleagues illustrates a difference between the meaning a sender intends and the tone a recipient can recover from text. The sender may have access to contextual knowledge or an imagined tone the recipient lacks. This is a reason to check important meanings, not a reason to assume text communication is inferior. Written communication can be accessible and useful, and it gives participants time to formulate a response.

Separate the cue, context, hypothesis and consequence of acting on it. Consider alternative explanations such as workload, familiarity, access needs, language, role and the task itself. Do not interpret communication differences as dishonesty, hostility or a personality defect. A pattern across relevant observations may warrant a question, but repetition does not establish motive. In low-stakes contexts, uncertainty may simply be left unresolved. When a cue includes a concrete threat or a documented safety requirement, act on the actual information rather than seeking a reassuring interpretation.

## Framework

**Observe–hypothesise–check–choose**

1. Record the words or action without adding a motive.
2. Identify context and at least two plausible explanations.
3. Ask whether the uncertainty matters to a legitimate decision.
4. If appropriate, seek a brief neutral clarification; otherwise leave the ambiguity unresolved.
5. Revise from new information. Use appropriate safety procedures for concrete risks.

Text model: observable cue → competing hypotheses → proportionate clarification or action. This is a reasoning aid, not a body-language decoder.

## Worked example

A fictional project partner replies 'OK' to a proposed handover time. The learner interprets it as resentment because the message has no greeting. Other possibilities include simple acceptance and time pressure. The immediate decision concerns whether the handover is agreed, not the partner's emotional state. A neutral confirmation is 'Thanks, I'll plan on Thursday at 2 unless the time changes.' If the partner says they meant only to acknowledge receipt, update the plan. No private explanation is needed. In a different case, a message stating a handover cannot occur would require a different action because the content itself changes the facts.

### Contrasting case

A fictional speaker avoids eye contact during a discussion but provides accurate answers. It would be unsupported to infer deception or disrespect from gaze. Communication styles and access needs vary, and the learner should evaluate the actual information rather than demand a particular performance of attentiveness.

## Guided practice

**Analyse three synthetic cues.** A: a delayed response to an optional invitation. B: a teammate says 'We should review this' after a presentation. C: an organiser sends an explicit cancellation. Record observation, two hypotheses where relevant, the decision affected and a next action.

**Worked solution.** A has multiple possible explanations; no response may be required, and a single low-pressure clarification may be suitable if planning depends on it. B could concern a routine check or a substantive concern; ask which issue needs review. C provides clear operational information: update the plan rather than speculate about the organiser's feelings. If cancellation affects an essential duty, verify the authorised alternative.

Now replace an accusation ('You ignored me') with an observation ('I haven't received confirmation') and a concrete question. The revised wording preserves the relevant fact without claiming knowledge of intent.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Identify a specific reasoning or communication correction and repeat using an equivalent fictional case. Assess observable task quality, not personality, popularity or another person's compliance.

# SOC-102 / L04 — Clarifying questions

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Evaluate and justify decisions about clarifying questions in the context of social awareness.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall the ambiguity and norm frameworks. A fictional colleague says a task is 'nearly ready'. You need to schedule a dependent task. What question would resolve the relevant uncertainty without interrogating them?

## Core teaching

A clarifying question seeks information that changes understanding or a legitimate decision. It differs from a leading question, which embeds an unverified conclusion; a loaded question, which presupposes a disputed fact; and interrogation, which demands more information than the purpose requires. A useful question is neutral, specific and proportionate. Open questions allow explanation; closed questions can verify a concrete detail. Neither format is inherently better.

Clarification has costs: it takes time, may interrupt others, and can request information a person prefers not to share. Ask when uncertainty affects a real decision, agreement, access need or safety concern. When the answer is not necessary, it may be better to leave ambiguity unresolved. In written communication, summarising an understood arrangement and inviting correction may be efficient. Perspective-taking research is a reminder that one's own interpretation may not match another person's perspective, not proof that every social exchange requires a question.

Power and context affect whether questions feel optional. A manager asking about a personal circumstance may be perceived differently from a friend asking about an optional plan. Minimise information requested, explain the legitimate purpose and permit refusal where appropriate. A request for confirmation of an agreed duty may be legitimate, but access to private motives or health information is not automatically needed. Credible danger requires the appropriate safety response rather than a prolonged questioning exercise.

## Framework

**Decision–gap–question–confirmation**

State the decision that needs information. Identify the smallest relevant gap. Choose an open or closed question without an unsupported assumption. Explain purpose if necessary, allow a suitable response format, and confirm the resulting understanding. If the person declines, work with available evidence or use an authorised route. Avoid repeating questions to obtain a preferred answer.

Example: 'When do you expect the table to be available? I need to schedule the verification.'

## Worked example

A fictional colleague says the report is nearly ready. Rather than ask, 'Why are you always late?', the learner asks whether the verified version will be ready before the agreed handover. The colleague explains that one figure is still missing. They agree to send the completed sections and flag the outstanding figure. This resolves the planning need without requiring private reasons. If the missing figure is essential, the learner records the dependency and communicates the revised timing through the authorised process. The original question was useful because its answer changed a decision.

### Contrasting case

A fictional acquaintance declines an optional invitation. The learner asks repeatedly why, hoping to establish whether the refusal means dislike. There is no legitimate need for the private explanation. Respect the refusal and avoid treating repeated questioning as social awareness.

## Guided practice

**Rewrite three questions.** A: 'Why are you ignoring my messages?' B: 'Don't you agree the new schedule is better?' C: 'What exactly is wrong with your health that you need a different format?' Use fictional contexts: a missing task confirmation, a proposed schedule change, and an access request.

**Worked solution.** A: 'Have you received the handover request, and can you confirm the agreed time?' B: 'What benefits or difficulties do you see with the proposed schedule?' C: 'Which format would allow you to access the materials?' For C, any actual documentation requirement belongs with the authorised process; do not ask for clinical details unnecessarily. Each revision removes a presupposition and targets information relevant to a decision.

Compare a closed confirmation of a meeting date with an open question about competing constraints. Explain why both may be appropriate in different circumstances.

Attempt before checking the worked solution. Identify a specific reasoning or communication correction and repeat using an equivalent fictional case. Assess observable task quality, not personality, popularity or another person's compliance.

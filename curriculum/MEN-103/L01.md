# MEN-103 / L01 — Avoidance

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Explain avoidance in the context of motivation and procrastination.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall how a habit cue differs from a goal. A fictional learner repeatedly delays submitting a draft while completing unrelated easy tasks. How would you tell whether the delay is avoidant, strategic or caused by a missing prerequisite?

## Core teaching

Procrastination is commonly understood as voluntary delay of an intended action despite anticipating that the delay will make matters worse. Not every postponement qualifies. Strategic sequencing, rest, unavailable resources, uncertainty about permission and changing priorities can justify delay. The distinction matters because blaming a learner for a genuine barrier will not remove the barrier. Treat the label as a description of behaviour in context, not a personality verdict.

One influential account proposes that procrastination can involve short-term emotion regulation: an unpleasant, uncertain or threatening task is postponed, and the immediate reduction in discomfort makes avoidance attractive. This is not the only explanation. Research also considers task aversiveness, expectations of success, delayed rewards and impulsiveness. The same behaviour may have different causes. For example, opening an easier task could reflect mood repair, legitimate prioritisation or insufficient information to begin. Ask what happened before the delay and what changed after it.

An avoidant loop can be represented as cue → discomfort or uncertainty → postponement → short-term relief → later cost. This is a teaching model, not a clinical explanation of every case. A useful response starts by checking whether the goal is still worthwhile and feasible. If it is, reduce an unnecessary barrier or choose a manageable first action. If the goal is unrealistic, negotiate it. Persistent severe difficulty, distress or impairment warrants support rather than repeated self-criticism.

## Framework

**Delay classification map**

1. Define the intended action and when it was feasible to start.
2. Identify what occurred before the delay: missing information, conflict of priorities, uncertainty, discomfort or other demand.
3. Record the immediate consequence of postponement and the later consequence.
4. Classify tentatively: justified delay, structural barrier, possible avoidance, or unclear/mixed.
5. Choose a proportionate response and define what evidence would change the classification.

The map is original instruction, not a psychological assessment.

## Worked example

A fictional project coordinator must prepare a short risk report by Friday. On Monday, they repeatedly reorganise folders instead of writing. The report is feasible and the coordinator anticipates greater pressure if it is delayed. They describe uncertainty about how to frame the opening section. A tentative avoidance explanation is that reorganisation offers immediate relief from uncertainty. A helpful experiment is to write an imperfect three-line outline, then ask a colleague whether it covers the required scope. If the colleague reveals that the report template was never supplied, revise the classification: a structural barrier also mattered.

### Contrasting case

The same coordinator postpones a non-urgent report because an essential safety issue requires attention. This is legitimate reprioritisation, not necessarily procrastination. Similarly, stopping to rest when unwell is not evidence of avoidance.

## Guided practice

### Classify four fictional delays

A: a form cannot be submitted because required access has not been granted. B: a learner repeatedly watches videos to avoid a manageable but uncomfortable draft and expects a later penalty. C: a task is moved to tomorrow after an urgent duty appears. D: a learner delays and is unsure why. For each, name a tentative classification and a next step.

### Worked solution

A: structural barrier; request access or an alternative. B: possible avoidance; test a small first action and clarify expectations. C: justified reprioritisation; communicate a new deadline. D: insufficient evidence; observe the task context, available resources and immediate consequences without inferring a disorder. The classification can change as information arrives.

Attempt the task before checking the worked solution. Identify one justified correction, then repeat using an equivalent fictional case. The criterion is quality of reasoning, not personal performance or symptom change.

## Independent transfer

Create two fictional delay chains with the same outward behaviour but different causes. Suggest a different response for each, and explain the evidence needed to distinguish them.

# EMO-102 / L01 — Trait models

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Explain trait models in the context of personality and temperament.

## Orientation and retrieval

Recall INT-104's distinction between describing an observed pattern and explaining its cause. A fictional colleague is talkative at work but quiet at home. Can one adjective capture their personality? The learning objective is to explain trait dimensions and temperament without reducing a person to a type.

## Core teaching

Personality refers to relatively characteristic patterns of feeling, thinking and behaviour. A trait is a descriptive dimension along which people can differ; it describes tendencies across occasions rather than a rule governing every action. The Five Factor Model groups many such tendencies into broad domains commonly called openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism (or negative emotionality). Different instruments use slightly different terminology. Openness concerns engagement with novel ideas and experience; conscientiousness includes organisation and goal-directed dependability; extraversion encompasses sociability and energetic engagement; agreeableness includes tendencies toward cooperation and consideration; negative emotionality concerns susceptibility to unpleasant emotional experiences. None of these dimensions is equivalent to a moral score, competence or a psychiatric diagnosis.

A domain summarises related but distinguishable facets. Soto and John's BFI-2, for example, organises fifteen narrower facets under five broader domains. A person might report being organised but not especially persistent; both patterns can inform a broad conscientiousness estimate without being identical. Treat scores as continuous summaries, not five boxes or rigid types. A descriptive model can assist discussion and prediction in some contexts but does not explain all motives, values, identity or developmental history. Different theoretical traditions may emphasise other characteristics or mechanisms.

Temperament commonly refers to early-emerging differences in reactivity and regulation; biology and development interact, and observed patterns are also shaped by environments and experience. A person's childhood tendencies do not determine an inevitable adult personality. Distinguish a trait from a temporary state, a learned skill and a chosen value. A quiet person can learn public speaking; an outgoing person can value solitude. A trait label is not sufficient to establish whether someone will be a good manager, partner or friend. Avoid using personality typologies as destiny, relationship compatibility formulas or employment selection instruments without evidence appropriate to that use.

## Framework

**Dimension–facet–evidence–limit map**

1. Describe a recurring observable pattern neutrally.
2. Identify one possible trait dimension and a narrower facet relevant to the pattern.
3. Record the situations in which the behaviour appears and does not appear.
4. Distinguish the description from explanations such as skill, role expectations or temporary stress.
5. State a limitation and an observation that would revise the inference.

Text model: multiple observations across contexts → tentative facet description → broad trait hypothesis. A reverse arrow from trait to inevitable behaviour is not justified. The map is an original exercise, not a psychometric measure.

## Worked example

A fictional volunteer coordinator regularly prepares checklists and submits paperwork on time, but sometimes changes plans at short notice to accommodate participants. The first observations may be compatible with conscientiousness, while flexibility reflects a different demand. It would be misleading to decide that the coordinator is 'a conscientious type' who will always prefer rigid plans. Consider alternative explanations: the role requires planning; an effective shared calendar supports it; the coordinator may be experienced in this work. Across a different fictional context, the coordinator forgets an optional event. This one exception does not refute a general tendency, but it reminds us that context and stakes matter. A fair description is narrower: the observed administrative tasks are consistently organised in this setting.

### Contrasting case

A fictional candidate speaks very little during a meeting conducted in a language they are still learning. It would be inappropriate to infer low extraversion from this observation alone. The task conditions differ from those in a conversation with familiar people using their preferred language.

## Guided practice

**Compare three fictional descriptions.** A: someone initiates conversations in familiar groups but not in unfamiliar formal settings. B: someone reliably meets agreed deadlines using reminders. C: someone reports being unsettled by criticism but returns to a routine after support. For each, suggest a possible dimension, a rival explanation and a limit.

**Worked solution.** A: sociability or assertiveness facets may be relevant, but familiarity and role expectations matter; do not infer a stable score. B: organisational or dependability tendencies may be relevant, but effective tools and clear expectations contribute. C: negative emotionality might be relevant in repeated observations, but one reaction has insufficient evidential scope. None establishes health status or competence. A strong answer identifies the observed pattern and an explicit competing explanation before using a trait term.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify one specific correction and apply it to an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning, evidence, safe boundaries and task quality rather than personal characteristics or emotional states.

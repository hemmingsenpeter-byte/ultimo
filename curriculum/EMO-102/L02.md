# EMO-102 / L02 — Situational variation

**Draft — independent academic and qualified subject review pending.** Outcome: Compare and distinguish situational variation in the context of personality and temperament.

## Orientation and retrieval

Retrieve the distinction between a trait and a state. Imagine a fictional adult who is expressive with friends, reserved in meetings and highly engaged when teaching a familiar topic. What changes across those situations?

## Core teaching

A personality state describes how a person is behaving, feeling or thinking at a particular time. A trait describes a broader tendency across relevant occasions. A useful perspective views traits partly through distributions of states: a person may exhibit a range of behaviours, with some occurring more often than others. Fleeson's experience-sampling studies examine this within-person variation. An average level and a range of observed states answer different questions. A high average does not mean that every observation is high, and similar averages can conceal different distributions.

Situations provide opportunities, incentives, norms and constraints. A person may be talkative while leading a workshop and quiet while listening to a grieving friend; these differences can be appropriate responses to the respective roles. The relationship also works in the other direction: people may select or shape situations in ways related to their interests or tendencies. Context includes familiarity, language, physical environment, accessibility, obligations, perceived safety and the stakes of a decision. Do not treat context as a nuisance to be ignored or assume every pattern is consciously chosen.

Separate observation from inference and inference from evaluation. A behaviour could be coded as initiating a conversation, but calling it confidence is already an interpretation. Judging the person as better or worse adds a value judgement. Repeated observations across settings may justify a more stable descriptive inference, but convenience samples may be unrepresentative. A week of observations during a demanding project may not represent ordinary circumstances. Small invented datasets can teach distributions and context without becoming instruments for evaluating real people.

## Framework

**State distribution and context comparison**

Define an observable behaviour and equal observation windows. Compare several fictional occasions across at least two contexts. Calculate a simple descriptive average and note variation; do not convert the result into a validated trait score. Identify one situational explanation and one alternative. Explain what additional evidence would be needed to infer a characteristic tendency.

Text visual: context A [1, 2, 1]; context B [4, 3, 4]. The overall average hides context differences. Specify what the numbers actually count.

## Worked example

Two fictional learners are observed during six comparable discussion opportunities. A initiates contributions 1, 2, 1 times in three unfamiliar meetings and 4, 3, 4 times in three familiar meetings. B initiates 2, 3, 2 and 3, 2, 3 times. Both total 15 contributions, averaging 2.5 per observation. A shows stronger context variation in this sample; B's counts vary less. Neither is necessarily more extraverted because initiating contributions is only one possible indicator, the cases are small, and meeting roles may differ. If A is the discussion leader in familiar meetings, role differences could explain some of the pattern. A fair conclusion reports the descriptive result rather than inventing trait percentiles.

### Contrasting case

Comparing ten contributions during a one-hour presentation with one contribution during a five-minute listening exercise does not establish situational personality change. Duration, expected role and opportunities differ; the raw counts are not comparable.

## Guided practice

**Analyse a synthetic observation log.** Fictional learner C initiates 0, 1, 2 contributions in three unfamiliar sessions and 3, 4, 2 in three familiar sessions. Compute means and identify two limitations. Then compare D, whose counts are 2, 2, 2 and 2, 2, 2.

**Worked solution.** C averages 1 in unfamiliar sessions and 3 in familiar sessions, overall 2. D averages 2 in both and overall 2. The same overall mean hides different context patterns. Limitations include small samples, varying roles and absence of a validated measurement instrument. A follow-up might compare equivalent roles across different settings, rather than assuming the observed change is a permanent trait difference.

Attempt before checking the model. Identify one specific correction and apply it to an equivalent fictional case. Assess reasoning, evidence, safe boundaries and task quality rather than personal characteristics or emotional states.

## Independent transfer

Construct a fictional six-observation dataset for a different behaviour, with two contexts and equal observation windows. Summarise average, range and contextual variation. Identify why the pattern may not generalise and how a better sampling design could reduce uncertainty.
